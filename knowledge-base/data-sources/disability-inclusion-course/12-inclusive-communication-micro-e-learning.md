### Video transcript: inclusive language and communication

Narrator Meghan:

Communication is a fundamental part of every workplace, and more importantly, every successful team. It fuels collaboration and innovation and enables us to bring our best ideas forward. But not every individual communicates in the same way. And workplaces that only use one traditional communication style often create unintended barriers.

For example, according to Texthelp, 56% of neurodivergent employees report facing communication barriers at work that affect their ability to contribute to their teams and carry out the responsibilities of their roles effectively. Inclusive communication is about making sure that what we want to convey and how we go about it is clear and accessible.

In this section we'll explore how utilising a variety of communication styles and inclusive communication practices, such as structured or chunked information, alternative formats, and accessible meeting options, can ensure that all colleagues feel heard, respected, and valued.

### Verbal Communication

Inclusive verbal communication is about understand the impacts that our word choices have on others. The language choices that we use reflect our assumptions and values, and they have the power to create safety and inclusion, or fear, shame, and isolation. Verbal communication can also provide clarity, motivation and success, or it can create confusion, misconception and stress.

If verbal communication is the only type of communication we rely on in the workplace, it could be excluding core groups of diversity, including deaf colleagues, neurodivergent colleagues, and colleagues who are new to the language.

There's also no paper trail if we solely rely on verbal communication, which means it can be easy to misunderstand, difficult to remember what was said, and there's no way to refer back to it for greater clarity.

There are some things we can do to make verbal communication more accessible:

Speak clearly and slowly, leaving time to process
Check the other person has understood and encourage questions
Enable captions and transcripts for verbal meetings
Follow up conversations with a written summary so each person can refer back to key points and actions

### Non-verbal communication (NVC)

We are all communicating, all of the time. Our bodies and facial expressions convey emotions, thoughts and meanings, and are an important form of communication in their own right. They can have a big impact on how inclusively we communicate, especially during difficult or potentially sensitive discussions around disability, access needs, and challenges within the workplace.

Closed NVC
You may seem closed and reluctant to talk if you:
Have a tense facial expression
Have your arms crossed and sit rigidly in your chair
Tap or move your feet as if agitated
Look distracted or check the clock as if your mind is elsewhere
Important: this may make it harder for colleagues or employees to share their experiences and challenges with you.

Open NVC
Instead, try maintaining open body language such as:
Keeping your arms and legs uncrossed
Keeping your face neutral when listening to difficult topics
Nodding reassuringly when someone is sharing something personal or vulnerable

## Text: subheading paragraph

### Written communication

Inclusive written communication means presenting information in clear, succinct, and well structured ways. When there's important announcements, instructions for tasks, or even written reports and policies, the essential insights and&nbsp;particulars can quickly get lost in a long block of dense text, or become jumbled and difficult to understand when mixed in with jargon and complex terminology. The good news, there are some really simple things we can do to make our written communications more accessible.

Read the key considerations below to learn more:

## Image: text aside

Image of a man walking while holding a stack of folders

- Bullet points are easier to read than long paragraphs

- Sans-serif fonts are less visually confusing than serif fonts

- Off-white backgrounds reduce visual strain

- Font size should be 12+ for body text and 18+ for titles

- Written documents should be available in a range of formats, including large text and accessibility checked to ensure they are compatible with assistive technology

- Top Tip for inclusion: Create a shared adjustments record, which should be a written document both people can refer back to, containing any information about adjustments made, what's working or not etc

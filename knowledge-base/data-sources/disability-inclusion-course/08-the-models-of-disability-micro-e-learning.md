The medical and charity models

Medical Model

### Video Transcript: The Social Model, Unlocking Potential in the Workplace

Narrator Meg:Imagine a workplace where talent thrives, everyone can contribute their unique skills and perspectives, and ideas and decisions benefit from diversity of thought. This is the power of the social model of disability, a framework that unlocks potential and fosters inclusion.

The social model was created by disabled activists in response to civil rights movements in the 1960s and 70s to challenge the idea that disability is solely defined by individual medical limitations.

The term Social Model of Disability was first coined by disabled academic Mike Oliver in 1983. Instead of focusing on individual impairment, the model focuses on the societal barriers: physical, attitudinal, communication, and systemic that prevent people from fully participating in all aspects of life, including the workplace.

In the workplace, the social model emphasises shifting the focus. It's not about fixing individuals, but about identifying and removing barriers. For example, instead of assuming someone with a mobility challenge can't do a specific task, provide accessible equipment and training.

Enabling employees. The social model encourages open communication and self-identification of disability. This means giving employees space to share their access needs and request adjustments that enable them to work effectively.

Building accessibility. From physical adaptations to inclusive communication practices, creating a truly accessible workplace benefits everyone, not just disabled people.

Embracing the social model is not just the right thing to do, it also makes business sense. When organisations reframe their understanding of disability to focus on removing barriers and supporting individuals, there are significant benefits.

These include:&nbsp;

- Talent acquisition. Organisations can access a wider pool of talent with diverse skills and experiences.

- Innovation. Fostering a culture of open communication and celebrating diversity of thought can lead to more creative perspectives and solutions.

- Employee engagement. By removing barriers and enabling and supporting employees. Organisations create a more positive and productive work environment for everyone.

- Reduced costs. Investing in accessibility can decrease long-term costs associated with inaccessible workplaces. The social model can provide a framework for tangible change.

While not all disabled people will follow the social model, as self-identification is a personal choice, the model is a fantastic starting place for identifying and removing access barriers in the workplace.

Some simple actions you could take to embrace the model include&nbsp;

- Speaking up. If you see or experience an accessibility barrier, share it and advocate for change.

- Start a conversation. Talk to your colleagues about disability inclusion. Make a plan for how your team can take steps to become more inclusive.

- Keep learning. Upskill on disability inclusion topics and engage with employee resource group events.

Throughout this short course, we will explore a range of common barriers, explore ways to create inclusive environments and provide you with further resources to embed and champion the social model in your role and team to create a more inclusive place to work, for everyone.

Understanding barriers

Now that you've watched the video on the social model, you will have learnt that the most important concept this framework suggests is that it is barriers in the environment that disable colleagues and prevent their active participation, not the individual's lived experience. 5 types of barriers

1: Physical
Obstacles limiting mobility and access

2: Environmental
Barriers in the built, digital and sensory environment

3: Communication
Barriers to accessing information or team collaboration

4: Systemic
Policies and practices that cause disadvantage

5: Attitudinal
Negative misconceptions and biases that create exclusion

If we can identify and remove these barriers from all aspects of our working environments, whether it's inaccessible buildings, unintentionally exclusionary policies, inflexible ways of working, or unconscious biases in our hiring practices, then we can create spaces where everyone has equitable opportunities to thrive and succeed.

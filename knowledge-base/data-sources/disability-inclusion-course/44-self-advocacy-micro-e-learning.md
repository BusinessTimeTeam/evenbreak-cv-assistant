### Video transcript: Introduction to self advocacy

Narrator Meghan:

Self-advocacy is all about being able to express our needs and request support. Yet many disabled and neurodivergent colleagues find it difficult to discuss access requirements with employers due to stigma, past negative experience, or workplace cultures that don't prioritise psychological safety.

This often leads to masking where employees hide the challenges they're facing, and this can create a high rate of burnout, sickness, absenteeism, or even presenteeism. An overwhelming 76% of disabled and neurodivergent employees fear sharing their diagnosis, lived experience, or access requests with an employer due to concerns around discrimination.

Such as being passed up for promotion or career progression, as well as apprehension about being seen as needy or seeking special treatment. In this section, we'll explore strategies for creating environments where self-advocacy is encouraged and supported. This includes clear communication of available resources, clear guidance on reasonable or workplace adjustments and accommodations, and fostering a culture where employees feel safe to speak up without fear of judgment. We'll also highlight practical tools, policies, and external resources that can enable colleagues to access the support they need while helping managers and teams respond effectively and inclusively.

Advocacy conversations
It can feel daunting having conversations with your manager/employer about your access needs, especially if there isn't currently an open culture of inclusion in your workplace. Here's some practical things you can do before and during the conversation to help:

Preparation:

Check any current adjustment processes the organisation has, and any policies, with Human Resources.

Identify and understand your barriers and your strengths, so that you can present both in a balanced way.

Have a think about what adjustments might help, or suggestions of what you would like to have happen.

Remain open minded and collaborative - your employer might have some great ideas to support you too.

Set your expectations and know what your rights are - research legal responsibility to provide adjustments.

During the conversation:

You can bring someone with you for support, whether it's a member of HR, a union representative, or a colleague.

Discussing sensitive and private information can sometimes be emotionally challenging - take a break if needed.

Use alternative communication methods if a call or face to face conversation is to difficult (e.g. email).

Focus on your barriers rather than your diagnosis.

Make sure you're clear on next steps by the end of the meeting, and make a written account of the agreed actions.

Top tip: You are entitled to a follow up call if your access needs change or the adjustments aren't working effectively.

## Text: subheading paragraph

### Encountering resistance

Your employer or manager shouldn't resist putting adjustments into place that help alleviate barriers or challenges you are facing. However, if they are, remind them of the following:

## List: bulleted

Adjustments are a legal right - each country has it's own laws to ensure rights for disabled people not to experience discrimination or disadvantage.

Only a court can decide if an adjustment is 'reasonable' - as long as your requested adjustments don't harm or negatively affect anyone else, there's no reason for your employer not to consider them.

Many changes don't cost anything - and there are often grants or govt funded budgets that can help organisations with more expensive adjustments.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following is the most important focus when having an advocacy conversation with your manager?

Correct answer! Focusing on barriers and their impact helps create constructive discussions and avoids unnecessary focus on medical details.

- Answer option: A. Your medical diagnosis in detail.

- Answer option: B. Complaining about previous managers.

- **Correct answer:** C. Your barriers and how they affect your work.

- Answer option: D. Comparing yourself to your colleagues.

- Answer option: C. Requesting adjustments without discussion.

### What should you do if your requested adjustments are not initially implemented?

Correct answer! Employees are entitled to adjustments by law, and reminding your employer while remaining collaborative can help achieve a resolution.

- Answer option: A. Accept it and do nothing.

- **Correct answer:** B. Remind your employer that adjustments are a legal right and discuss possible solutions.

- Answer option: C. Cancel all future meetings with your manager.

- Answer option: D. Only request adjustments in writing and never follow up.

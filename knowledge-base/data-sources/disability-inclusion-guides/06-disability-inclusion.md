# Disability inclusion

Disability Inclusion 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak 

Introduction 
Disability in history 
For much of history, disabled people were excluded, institutionalised, or treated as objects of pity rather than equal members of society. The negative and limiting perceptions that entrenched society led to systemic discrimination, restricted opportunities, and environments designed without disabled people in mind. 
Many of the after effects of this dated and oppressive mindset towards disability can still be seen in our modern day societies, in inadequate public services, including inaccessible public transport, housing, and healthcare, as well as in ableist attitudes in education and work sectors; creating huge gaps in education and employment statistics between disabled and non-disabled people. 

Attitudes began to shift during the 20th century, as disabled activists worldwide pushed for recognition, accessibility, and rights. Major political shifts globally, including the civil rights movement, and the fight for legalisation of same sex marriages, spurred activists to take a stand. 

Landmark legislation, such as the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) and the UK Equality Act (2010), alongside global frameworks like the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), marked major steps toward equality. While barriers and discrimination still exist, disability rights movements have transformed the conversation from pity and limitation to inclusion, equity, and empowerment, with increasing recognition that society must adapt—not individuals. 

Why is disability inclusion important? 
As you will have learned in the introductory e-learning, disability is not really a minority group. In fact, it's a large percentage of the population, with roughly 1 in 6 people globally experiencing some form of disablement. That's a huge number of people, and that number doesn't include people who are still seeking support to obtain a diagnosis and sufficient support.
It's also the only marginalised identity that anyone can join at any point in their life, whether through age affecting things like vision or hearing, through an accident that creates physical mobility challenges, or through a long term health condition that creates chronic pain or fatigue. 
It's therefore incredibly vital that we as a society understand disability as a form of individual and cultural identity, and one that comes with many unique skills, insights and assets. 
This is especially important in the workplace, where studies globally have found that having diverse and disabled employees in a workplace comes with numerous benefits: 
According to Scope (UK), companies that are leading in disability inclusion report 72% higher productivity, 45% better workplace safety, 30% higher profit margins, and 200% higher net income compared to peers
In EY Japan, neurodivergent professionals in certain roles were found to be ~30% more productive than teams without them, with higher engagement and better morale among neurodivergent staff
Workplaces with disability inclusion often have heightened awareness of health, safety, and environmental risks—since disabled people are more sensitive or aware to issues like physical hazards or accessibility barriers. Australasia WIR notes that disabled employees often bring stronger awareness of safety issues, which can strengthen risk management culture

Despite the statistics and evidence that disability and diversity in all of its forms are a benefit and an asset in the workplace, diversity is still stigmatised and unsupported in many workplaces. 

In studies across the UK: 
3 in 4 people said they have experienced negative attitudes or behaviours towards their disability in the last 5 years 
90% reported that discriminatory attitudes towards disability have had negative effects on their life 
33% said others have made negative assumptions or judgements about their capability 
25% of people have been accused of faking their lived experiences or not really being disabled 
60% of employees who have been given workplace adjustments still don't have everything they need to complete their job roles successfully 
34% didn't request adjustments at all, because they feared being ostracised, seen as a burden, or judged as needing 'special treatment'

So, how do we change this? 

Disability inclusion is about creating workplace cultures where people can be honest about their different experiences and needs, without fear of judgement, shame or exclusion. Whether you identify as disabled or neurodivergent yourself, know of colleagues who do, or have a new team member joining who has different access needs, it's important that every member of the team understands, respects, and celebrates differences. 

Here's 3 key concepts to understand about disability that will help you get started: 

1. The individual is the expert in their own experience! 
Disability inclusion starts with recognising that people know themselves best. You don't need private medical details to provide support — focusing on diagnoses can create barriers. Instead, listen to what individuals share, respect their preferences, and collaborate on solutions. This builds trust and ensures support is tailored, practical, and empowering. 
2. Everyone's experience is different 
No two people experience disability in the same way, even with a similar diagnosis. Factors like race, gender, sexuality, socioeconomic background, and culture all shape how individuals encounter barriers in society — a concept known as intersectionality. This means support should never be a "one size fits all" approach. 

By staying open-minded and flexible, you can better understand the diverse experiences within your team and provide support that respects each person's unique situation. 

3. Flexibility benefits everyone 
Encouraging different ways of working, including making workplace adjustments, isn't about giving "special treatment"—it's about levelling the playing field and removing barriers so everyone has equal opportunity to thrive. Simple changes such as sharing agendas in advance, offering flexible working arrangements, or ensuring communication is clear and accessible make the workplace better for all employees, not just disabled colleagues. When inclusion is built into everyday practices, it fosters productivity, wellbeing, and stronger collaboration across the whole team.

Quick wins for leaders 
When you create an environment where disabled voices are heard and championed, where lived experience is valued and celebrated, and where all employees are supported to thrive, you will quickly attract diversity through the door, and begin seeing the innovation and high performance that comes with it. 

Here's a quick 6 step plan for increasing inclusion in your workplace: 

Step 1: Build awareness and understanding 
Provide training on equity vs. equality, the impact of bias, and how intersectional barriers affect colleagues differently. 

Step 2: Foster open conversations 
Create safe, judgment-free spaces where employees feel comfortable sharing needs, preferences, or barriers they encounter. 

Step 3: Offer adjustments proactively 
Normalise flexible adjustments (e.g., flexible hours, quiet workspaces, accessible documents) without requiring a formal diagnosis. 

Step 4: Encourage diverse working styles 
Value and support different approaches to communication, collaboration, and problem-solving. 

Step 5: Review systems and practices 
Audit hiring, promotion, and feedback processes to identify and remove barriers that may disadvantage disabled or neurodivergent staff. 

Step 6: Embed continuous improvement 
Regularly gather feedback, monitor progress, and adapt policies and practices to ensure ongoing accessibility and inclusion.

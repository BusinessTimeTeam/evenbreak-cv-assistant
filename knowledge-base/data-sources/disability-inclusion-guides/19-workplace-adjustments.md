# Workplace adjustments

Implementing Workplace Adjustments
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Workplace adjustments (sometimes called accommodations or modifications) are changes that remove barriers and enable disabled colleagues to perform their roles effectively. They are not "special treatment"—they are about equity, ensuring everyone has fair access to work and opportunities. Adjustments can be simple, low-cost, and highly impactful, yet many employees face delays or stigma when requesting them. Creating a proactive, transparent process helps reduce stress, increase productivity, and build trust.
What Workplace Adjustments Can Look Like
Adjustments vary depending on individual needs, role, and environment. They may include:
	•	Physical changes – step-free access, adjustable desks, ergonomic equipment, quiet rooms.
	•	Technology supports – screen readers, dictation software, hearing loops, captioning tools.
	•	Flexible working – remote or hybrid options, flexible hours, extra breaks.
	•	Communication and process changes – plain language documents, interpreters, visual task boards.
	•	Support services – job coaches, workplace strategy coaching, or assistance animals.
No two employees will need the same combination of adjustments—what matters is personalisation and collaboration.
Key Principles for Implementing Adjustments
	•	Listen first – The employee is the expert on their own needs. Create space for them to explain what works best.
	•	Act quickly – Long delays in providing adjustments create barriers and frustration. Streamlined processes are essential.
	•	Keep it flexible – Needs can change over time due to health, environment, or role changes. Adjustments should be reviewed regularly.
	•	Protect privacy – Employees should not need to disclose detailed medical information to justify adjustments. Focus on what support is required, not why.
	•	Normalise adjustments – Everyone benefits from flexibility and inclusive design. Treat adjustments as routine, not exceptional.
Common Barriers in Adjustment Processes
	•	Overly medicalised systems – Requiring proof of diagnosis can delay or block access.
	•	Inconsistent policies – Different managers applying different standards leads to unfairness.
	•	Budget concerns – Assumptions that adjustments are costly can cause resistance, even though most adjustments are inexpensive or free.
	•	Stigma and fear – Employees may avoid requesting support due to fear of discrimination or being seen as a burden.
Implementing adjustments
Guidance for leadership
Look for 'one size fits all' 
Sweeping statements in policies that apply to all employees and are likely to be excluding some groups or individuals. For example, recruitment policies that mandate interviews may disadvantage anxious or neurodiverse people. Challenge this by suggesting including alternative methods like recorded responses or written tasks.
Review your wording carefully 
The language you use in policies has a big impact. For example, a statement like: "Employees who take frequent or long-term sick leave will be subject to disciplinary procedures" can discourage people from being open about their needs, especially when those needs relate to disability, neurodivergence, or mental health.
Instead, use more supportive language, such as:
"We generally provide X number of days for absences, but we are committed to working with employees to understand their individual needs and find fair, flexible, and inclusive solutions."
This kind of wording helps create psychological safety and reassures employees that they can request time off or adjustments without fear of being penalised.
Model inclusive alternatives in your own team 
Be the change you want to see in your workplace. Trial inclusive practices, including flexible hours, different ways of working and engaging etc. Document what works, with evidence, and then share with other leaders and policy makers. You can then advocate for changes to be made in policy review or strategy planning days.
Talk to your employees! 
Policies are often written without diverse perspective or input. As a leader, you can change that. Seek feedback from your employees, in safe spaces and anonymous feedback forms, and then use this to inform how you question the systems in place, not just for those with an official diagnosis, but for every employee.
Courageous conversations for leaders
Practical advice for discussing reasonable adjustments with your teams
Talking about adjustments for disability, neurodivergence, and mental health can be a little daunting if you've never done it before, but that makes it all the more important! Here's a six step process you can use as the framework for having courageous conversations with your employees, to ensure they get the support that they need.
Step 1: Clearly express your intent
Whether you're offering support, addressing a concern, or seeking clarity, clearly stating your intent at the start of a courageous conversation helps build trust and set a respectful tone. E.g. clearly stating 'I'd like to talk to you about X' will reduce anxiety and any chance of misunderstanding
Step 2: Make sure the environment is safe and comfortable for both people
These discussions can sometimes be very personal, and should be held somewhere private. Ask 'is this a good time to have this conversation? Where would you feel most comfortable to talk about X?'
Step 3: Listen actively
That means leaving any assumptions at the door, and giving the other person your undivided attention. Just listening to someone, without the need to respond or fix things, can help someone feel really valued and supported.
Step 4: Ask open questions
This allows the conversation to be led by the individual, who is the expert on their own experiences and the adjustments they need. Give them plenty of time and space to open up about the barriers they are facing.
Step 5: Role model
Demonstrating that we are all human, that it's okay to make mistakes, and that you need support sometimes too, sets the tone for your team in these courageous conversations, and helps build safety and trust.
Step 6: Be accountable for your actions
Follow up on the things that you commit to. Inclusion isn't about what we say, it's about what we do, so if you say you'll do something, do it. You don't have to have all of the answers, but you can let the other person know you'll do your best to find the answers out. And if you get things wrong, that's okay too. Own it, apologise, and learn from it for next time.
Revisiting adjustments
What to do if adjustments aren't working
Adjustments aren't one-size-fits-all and they're often not a quick fix. An employee may be facing challenges but unsure what support would help. Finding the right adjustments can take time, and needs may change as roles, environments, or circumstances shift. It's often a process of trial and error.
Here are three golden rules for reviewing and updating adjustments effectively:
Rule 1: Have an open follow up conversation. Some employees may feel uncomfortable admitting that something isn't working - remind them that it's not a failure or a burden, just part of an ongoing process to find what's right for them.
Rule 2: Build support step by step. If you're unsure what kind of support someone needs, start small. Trial simple adjustments, like using a different communication method or project management tool, and see how they help.
If more support is needed, build on that foundation. For example: "Let's try this approach first, and if it's not enough, we can explore coaching or extend your deadlines."
Layer support gradually, based on ongoing feedback and what works best for the individual.
Rule 3: Encourage the employee to document their progress. This should be a confidential, shared record between the two of you, so that you can both see what's been tried and what's improving or staying the same. It's important to frame this document as a reflective space to create continual support, and not a way to monitor the employee or their performance.

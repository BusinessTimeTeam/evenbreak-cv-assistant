# Supporting mental wel

Supporting mental wellbeing 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak 

Introduction 
Mental wellbeing is a vital part of a healthy, productive workplace. Just as we look after physical health, it’s important to recognise and support mental health as an everyday priority. When organisations take steps to create open, understanding cultures, they not only reduce stigma but also help people feel valued, safe, and able to thrive. 
This guide offers practical insights on recognising when someone might be struggling, building confidence in starting conversations, and understanding common mental health conditions. It also provides simple tips for supporting colleagues and promoting positive wellbeing. 
By embedding these practices, we can move towards workplaces where mental health is respected, challenges are met with compassion, and everyone feels supported to bring their best selves to work. 
Why Mental Health in the Workplace Matters? 
We all have mental health, just as we all have physical health. At different points in our lives, we might be thriving, coping, or struggling – and this can shift over months, days, or even moments. 
It’s important to remember that just because you can’t see something doesn’t mean it isn’t real or painful. Sometimes, our mental health challenges show through our behaviours, but sometimes they remain invisible to others. 
Mental health sits on a continuum. At any given moment, a person may have a mental health diagnosis and be doing well, while another without a diagnosis may be struggling significantly. 
Optimal mental wellbeing 
Someone who has been diagnosed with a mental health condition, yet experiences high levels of mental well-being 
Diagnosis 
Someone who has been diagnosed with a mental health condition, and experience low levels of mental wellbeing 
No Diagnosis
Someone who has no diagnosis of a mental health condition and experience high levels of mental wellbeing 
Minimal mental wellbeing 
Someone who has no diagnosis of a mental health condition, yet experiences low levels of mental wellbeing 
Four quadrants illustrate that:
	•	A person can have a diagnosis and still experience high wellbeing.
	•	A person can have no diagnosis and high wellbeing.
	•	A person can have a diagnosis and low wellbeing.
	•	A person can have no diagnosis and low wellbeing.
So, why is it important?
The workplace environment plays a crucial role in shaping where people sit on the mental health continuum.
Other factors include both individual factors (e.g., traumatic events, genetic predisposition, access to support, substance use, emotional sensitivity, etc.) and societal factors (e.g., isolation, unemployment, poverty, inequality, discrimination, etc.)
According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), around 1 in 4 people will experience a mental health challenge in any given year.
Additionally, only about half of employees feel comfortable talking about mental health at work (The Harris Poll) – and this varies hugely between cultures, whether that's at a workplace, a local, national, or regional level.
In the UK, 25% say their job has a negative impact on their mental health (CIPD Good Work Index 2025).
And globally, an estimated 12 billion working days are lost every year due to mental health challenges, through both absenteeism (having to take time off work) and presenteeism (being at work but unable to function effectively).
These figures highlight why building a culture that supports mental health is essential for individuals, teams and organisations as a whole.

Recognising When Someone Might Be Struggling
Warning signs vary hugely from person to person and context to context, but common indicators include:
	•	Sudden or noticeable changes in behaviour or performance.
	•	Risky or impulsive actions.
	•	Dark comments, jokes, direct talk about death, suicidal thoughts or plans, and/or expression of hopelessness or feeling like a burden.
	•	Visible shifts in appearance, energy levels, or body language.
	•	Ongoing disengagement and/or withdrawal from work, spending time with colleagues, or usual activities once enjoyed.
	•	Difficulty concentrating or a noticeable drop in performance
Spotting these early can make it easier to provide support before things worsen. So, if you are concerned about someone, start the conversation. It's better to be wrong and told all is well than to regret not asking.

Talking about Mental Health
Conversations about mental health can feel daunting, especially in the workplace. Yet creating an environment where these conversations are encouraged and taking the first step to talk can make a huge difference. In some cases, it can even save a life.
When starting a conversation:
	•	Set the scene – Choose a private and safe environment for both parties.
	•	Be clear about your intention – For example, "I'd like to understand how you're doing and how I can support you."
	•	Listen actively – Show that you're engaged, avoid interrupting, and reflect back what you've heard.
	•	Thank them for sharing – It takes courage to open up and be vulnerable.
	•	Ask what they need – Don't assume you know best. You don't, they do.
	•	Follow through – If you agree on actions, stick to them and check in regularly.
Mental Health Conditions
Mental health conditions are varied and can co-occur. The DSM-5 (The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition ) outlines different diagnoses.
Each condition has its own criteria, but experiences differ, so it's important to focus on individuals rather than labels.
Here are some examples of mental health conditions:
Anxiety disorders 
Involve excessive worry, fear, or panic that can interfere with daily activities and concentration. These may include generalised anxiety disorder, social anxiety, or panic disorder.
Bipolar disorder 
Characterised by shifts between depressive episodes and periods of elevated mood or energy (mania or hypomania), which can impact stability and routine.
Depression 
More than just low mood, depression can cause persistent feelings of sadness, hopelessness, and loss of interest, affecting energy, sleep, and work performance.
Eating disorders 
Such as anorexia, bulimia, or binge-eating disorder. They involve having a difficult relationship with food and body image and can have serious physical as well as mental health effects.
Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) 
Involves recurring, intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviours (compulsions) aimed at reducing distress or preventing feared events.
Personality disorders 
A group of conditions where long-standing patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving can cause distress or difficulty in relationships. We will deep dive into these further in this guide.
Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) 
Develops after experiencing or witnessing traumatic events, leading to flashbacks, hypervigilance, and avoidance behaviours that affect daily life.
Schizophrenia and related conditions 
Involve disruptions in thinking, perception, and behaviour, which may include hallucinations, delusions, or disorganised speech.
Top tips
Here are top tips on how to support a colleague or team member who is currently struggling with their mental health. Each person's experiences and needs will be different, so see these as a starting point, not an exhaustive checklist.
	•	Encourage conversations about mental health. The more common they become, the less stigma remains.
	•	Offer flexibility of work location, hours, breaks, and workload. Offer time off for mental health reasons.
	•	Listen without judgment. Show care beyond work tasks and targets by checking in regularly, seeking to understand, and asking open-ended questions.
	•	Don't make assumptions. Experiences vary from one individual to another and from day to day for the same person.
	•	Support, don't diagnose. Your role isn't to play clinician. Focus on understanding needs, providing support, and signposting to resources.
	•	Be consistent, honest, and clear. Reliability helps build trust.
	•	Validate emotions. Don't dismiss or judge them, even if they seem intense to you – remember, everyone feels differently.
	•	Suggest professional help. When appropriate, signpost to support services. Ask the person whether they are open to it before going ahead.
	•	Give feedback carefully. Choose the right moment and balance constructive feedback with encouragement.
	•	Use inclusive language. Avoid casual use of mental health diagnoses such as "I'm a bit OCD" or "You're so borderline".

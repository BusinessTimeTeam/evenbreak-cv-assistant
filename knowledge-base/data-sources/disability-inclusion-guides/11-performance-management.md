# Performance management

Performance Management 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak

Introduction
Performance management is often seen as a standard business process. However, for disabled and neurodivergent team members it can be unintentionally exclusionary if not designed and delivered effectively. Inclusive performance management not only ensures fairness, it also unlocks the potential of talent that might otherwise be overlooked.
Focus on outcomes
Not just progress
Disabled and neurodivergent team members may achieve results in different ways. The focus should be on outcomes and impact, rather than a particular way of working. Flexibility in how objectives are met ensures that talent is recognised for value delivered.
Co-create clear objectives
Collaboratively setting goals helps ensure that expectations are realistic and aligned to individual strengths. Objectives should be clear, unambiguous and achievable, with any agreed adjustments in place. How they will be measured also needs to be agreed. Co-creation builds accountability and trust, while reducing anxiety about unclear or shifting standards.
Prioritise strengths and potential
Disabled and neurodivergent team members often bring exceptional problem-solving, creativity, or analytical skills. Performance discussions should celebrate these contributions and explore how roles can be adapted to include them. This strength-based approach encourages engagement and retention.
Make feedback a dialogue
Feedback works best when it is a two-way conversation. Encouraging employees to share what helps them perform at their best, and what barriers they face, creates a culture of openness. This turns performance management from a one-off event into a continuous development partnership.
Embed workplace adjustments
Adjustments such as alternative feedback formats, assistive technology, flexible scheduling, or additional preparation time should be built into performance processes. Reviews should check not only what has been achieved but also whether the team member's access needs are being met.
Train managers
Diversity awareness training
Difficulties often stem from a lack of understanding. Equipping managers with the knowledge to recognise diverse communication styles, processing differences, or hidden barriers helps them evaluate performance fairly, and provide constructive feedback without misinterpretation.
Manage performance objectively
Sometimes, there is a reluctance to manage the performance of disabled or neurodivergent team members, but this can unfairly limit their opportunities to grow and develop. If someone's performance is below reasonable expectations, and you know you have ensured all their access needs have been met, it is fine to set up a performance improvement plan, and evaluate performance in the same way, and with the same consequences, you might a non-disabled team member.
Psychological safety
Create psychological safety so employees feel comfortable discussing what works for them Invite open dialogue, frame feedback as mutual learning, and reassure employees that you want to understand their needs and help them succeed.
Avoid vague language
Be specific and clear
Phrases like "more proactive" or "we'd like to see better communication" are unclear and open to misinterpretation. Use specific examples relating to particular projects or instances.
Clarity reduces anxiety and helps everyone understand expectations.
Check your bias
Reflect on how your own lens may be shaped by neurotypical norms. We all carry assumptions based on our own experiences. Challenge your own expectations of what "good performance" looks like.
Reframing your approach
Think of feedback as a shared learning process, not a one-way critique.
For example:
"I noticed [specific behaviour or outcome]. Can you talk me through your experience?"
Then listen.
There may be an adjustment, context, or misunderstanding that changes how the behaviour or action is perceived.
"Thank you for explaining, that helps me understand your perspective. Here's how I initially interpreted it... How can we work together to ensure you have everything you need going forward?"
This turns feedback into a two-way, inclusive dialogue and one that builds trust, clarity, and mutual understanding.
Inclusive performance management
Identifying the types of support your employees need
Before any performance review, consider whether the employee might be facing unmet access needs. Barriers can exist with or without a formal diagnosis or disclosure.
Focus the review on identifying the challenge, understanding its impact, and exploring practical solutions together. The following clean feedback framework can help guide that conversation:
Situation
Start with describing to your employee the context and the situation that you think may be a barrier or challenge e.g. 'Last week, I noticed that your report for X client was handed in late.'
Behaviour
Next, describe your observations on the specific behaviour, remaining objective and avoiding judgmental or accusatory language. E.g. 'I have observed that there's been a few missed deadlines over the past two weeks.'
Impact
Clearly explain the impact of the behaviour or challenge to help the employee understand the broader effects and work together on solutions.
E.g. "The impact is that other team members have had to reshuffle their own priorities to ensure the reports are submitted on time. Is there anything you're finding challenging at the moment, whether it's workload, something outside of work, or anything else? If so, I'd really like us to explore ways to make things easier for you."
Using this approach shows there's no blame; it's okay to struggle, and as their manager, you're there to support them.
Give the employee time to process and respond. Listen carefully to their concerns and perspective, then work together to identify next steps, whether that's an adjustment, communication support, training, or more regular check-ins.
Coaching techniques for inclusive performance management
Coaching is a practice that encourages self-directed progress, and helps employees to achieve their personal and career goals, navigate challenges in the workplace, introduce new ways of thinking about tasks, and develop their skills.
You don't have to be a trained coach in order for coaching techniques to benefit your employees, but you can use coaching techniques in your performance reviews and courageous conversations. Here's a great coaching framework to help set proactive next steps, and encourage your employees to take initiative and autonomy over their futures:
(G) Goals - Start by setting some goals with your employee. Goals are always more likely to be achieved when the employee has helped co- create them, and they should be achievable, measurable, and have a rough time period attached for when the goal will be reviewed.
(R) Reality - Address the reality of how they are currently performing in their role, in a sensitive and supportive manner, and also ask about the reality of any challenges they may be facing.
(O) Options - During this stage, you want your employee to identify any resources they have that they may be under-utilising, and any solutions or possibilities that would help them overcome the challenges they are facing. The employee should take a very active role in this section, so give them plenty of time to process and contribute ideas before jumping in.
(W) Way forward - End the review by agreeing on next steps that create a clear and well planned way forward, for the employee to work towards the goals that have been set. You can then add regular one to one check in's with the employee to help them make continual progress towards the goal and make sure they have everything they need to thrive.
When using the GROW model, try these powerful coaching questions to help expand thinking, unlock insight, and support practical next steps, for both you and your employee:
	•	What did you do differently today that worked?
	•	What assumptions are you making about yourself and others, that might be preventing you from moving forward?
	•	What are you not doing, or what am I not doing to support you, that would help you make progress?
	•	Is there anything we haven't addressed that you would regret not talking about?

"There are a lot of similarities between great coaching, and inclusive leadership. Both are a balance between being the right amount of professional, and the right amount of vulnerable.
It's about being genuinely caring, and acting as a pivot point for people to find support, empowerment, and redirection."

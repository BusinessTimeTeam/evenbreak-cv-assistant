# Understanding different

Understanding Different Neurotypes
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Neurodivergence is an umbrella term that encompasses a wide variety of experiences, known as different 'neurotypes'. In this guide, we'll break down some of these experiences, common barriers and ways of working for a variety of neurotypes, a well a providing some inclusive top tips and best practice guidance for supporting different neurotypes in the workplace.
This is a non-exhaustive list, because these are many diverse ways of processing, thinking, learning, and working, but this guide will cover:
	•	Autism and ADHD
	•	Bipolar disorder
	•	Dyslexia and Dysgraphia
	•	Dyscalculia and Dyspraxia
	•	Tourette's and tic disorders
Bipolar disorder
What is BPD?
BPD is a mental health condition that impacts the way people think, feel, and relate to others. It's often misunderstood and carries a lot of stigma. Yet, with the right support in place, people who live with BPD can thrive personally and professionally, bringing richness and strengths to the workplace.
How it is defined
According to the DSM-5, BPD is diagnosed when a person meets at least five out of nine criteria, which include:
	•	Frantic efforts to avoid real or perceived abandonment.
	•	Intense or unstable relationships.
	•	Shifting sense of self or identity.
	•	Impulsive and sometimes risky behaviours.
	•	Suicidality and/or self-harm.
	•	Emotional instability.
	•	Intense or uncontrolled anger.
	•	Chronic feelings of emptiness.
	•	Stress-related paranoia or losing touch with reality.
Diversity of experience
Many also have co-occurring disabilities, other mental health conditions, and/or neurodivergence. For example:
	•	84% of individuals with BPD also have at least one other mental health condition, approximately.
	•	30% of individuals with BPD also have ADHD, approximately.
Not everyone with BPD will experience the same challenges. And challenges will vary within the same person depending on the context, as well as day by day or even hour by hour. An individual may be thriving one day and struggling the next.
The BPD community is extremely diverse, and everyone is unique, so do not make any assumptions based on our own or other people's experiences.
Supporting BPD in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Provide flexibility with deadlines or working hours during periods of low or high energy.
	•	Encourage open conversations about preferred ways of working, without pressuring disclosure.
	•	Offer quiet, low-stimulation spaces for focus and rest.
	•	Agree on clear priorities to help avoid overwhelm during periods of fluctuating energy.
	•	Check in regularly and sensitively, without making assumptions about someone's capability. 
ADHD
What is Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder?
Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental difference that affects attention, focus, memory, and self-regulation. It is often stereotyped as "hyperactive children," but ADHD presents in many different ways across all ages. With the right understanding and support, people with ADHD can thrive, bringing creativity, problem-solving, and fresh perspectives to the workplace.
How it is defined
According to the DSM-5, ADHD is diagnosed when a person shows persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interfere with functioning. Symptoms must have been present before age 12, occur in two or more settings, and cause significant difficulties.
The DSM-5 identifies three presentations:
	•	Predominantly inattentive presentation (e.g. difficulty sustaining attention, disorganisation, forgetfulness).
	•	Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive presentation (e.g. fidgeting, restlessness, impulsive actions).
	•	Combined presentation (a mix of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive traits).
Examples of diagnostic criteria include:
	•	Frequently making careless mistakes or missing details.
	•	Difficulty sustaining attention in tasks or conversations.
	•	Often losing items necessary for daily activities.
	•	Trouble organising tasks and managing time.
	•	Restlessness or difficulty remaining seated.
	•	Talking excessively or blurting out answers.
	•	Interrupting or intruding on others.
Diversity of experience
Not everyone with ADHD experiences the same challenges. Difficulties may vary depending on the context — for instance, someone may struggle with detail-heavy tasks but excel in high-pressure or creative environments. Symptoms can also fluctuate day by day, influenced by factors like sleep, stress, and environment.
The ADHD community is incredibly diverse, and every person's experience is unique. Avoid making assumptions based on stereotypes or limited examples.
Supporting ADHD in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Break down tasks into manageable steps with clear, written instructions.
	•	Allow the use of productivity tools such as reminders, calendars, or project trackers.
	•	Encourage movement breaks and flexible working environments.
	•	Reduce distractions in shared spaces where focus is needed.
Autism
What is autism?
Autism is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference that shapes the way people perceive, process, and interact with the world. It is not an illness to be cured but a natural form of human diversity. Autistic people can bring deep focus, honesty, innovation, and unique perspectives to their work and relationships, particularly when supported in environments that recognise and value neurodiversity.
How it is defined
According to the DSM-5, autism is characterised by two core areas of difference:
	•	Social communication and interaction – for example, differences in nonverbal communication, understanding social dynamics, or building and maintaining relationships.
	•	Restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities – for example, strong focus on specific topics, routines, sensory sensitivities, or repetitive movements.
The DSM-5 describes autism as a spectrum, meaning it can present in many different ways and at varying levels of support need. No two autistic people are alike.
Diversity of experience
Autism is often described as a "spectrum," but this does not mean a straight line from "mild" to "severe." Instead, think of it as a profile of strengths and challenges that looks different for each person. Some may need significant daily support, while others may be highly independent — both are equally autistic.
Autistic experiences also intersect with gender, race, culture, sexuality, and socio-economic background. For example, autistic women, non-binary people, and people of colour are often underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed because autism research and diagnostic tools have historically centred on white boys.
Many autistic people also experience co-occurring conditions such as:
	•	ADHD
	•	Anxiety and/or depression
	•	Epilepsy
	•	Learning differences (e.g., dyslexia or dyspraxia)
	•	Sensory processing differences
It's also important to remember that autism is not only about challenges. Autistic individuals often excel in areas such as:
	•	Pattern recognition and detail focus
	•	Creative problem-solving
	•	Deep knowledge and expertise in areas of interest
	•	Strong sense of fairness and integrity
Context and community
Autistic traits may appear more or less visible depending on the environment. Someone might thrive in a calm, structured setting but struggle in noisy or unpredictable spaces. Many autistic people also engage in masking — hiding or suppressing traits in order to "fit in" — which can be exhausting and contribute to stress or burnout.
The autistic community itself is broad and diverse. People may identify differently (e.g.,"Autistic person" or "person with autism"), and language preferences should always be respected. There is no single autistic experience, so it is important to listen to individuals and avoid assumptions based on stereotypes or limited examples.
Supporting Autism in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Communicate clearly and avoid vague or ambiguous instructions - be specific.
	•	Provide advance notice of changes in routine, meetings, or deadlines.
	•	Offer sensory-friendly spaces and adjustable lighting, noise, or seating, or offer work from home options.
	•	Respect preferred communication styles, whether written, verbal, or visual.
	•	Provide clear feedback that is specific and actionable, rather than general.
	•	Respect and value special interests, which can bring deep expertise to projects.
Dyslexia and Dysgraphia
What are they?
Dyslexia and dysgraphia are specific learning differences that affect how people process written language.
	•	Dyslexia primarily impacts reading, spelling, and word recognition
	•	Dysgraphia primarily impacts handwriting, spelling, and written expression.
Both are unrelated to intelligence and do not reflect a person's ability to think, create, or succeed. In fact, many people with these differences bring unique strengths, such as creative problem-solving, visual thinking, and storytelling.
How it is defined
According to the DSM-5, both fall under the category of Specific Learning Disorders:
	•	Dyslexia is described as difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition, poor decoding, and spelling challenges.
	•	Dysgraphia (while less explicitly named in the DSM-5, it is recognised within written expression difficulties) involves challenges in handwriting, spelling, grammar, punctuation, and organising ideas in written form.
Examples of features include:
	•	Differences in reading fluency or decoding unfamiliar words.
	•	Frequent spelling errors, even with familiar words.
	•	Slow, effortful, or messy handwriting.
	•	Trouble organising written work or translating ideas into writing.
	•	Avoidance of tasks that involve extended reading or writing.
Diversity of experience
No two people with dyslexia or dysgraphia will experience them in the same way. Some may read fluently but struggle with spelling. Others may have strong spoken communication but find writing exhausting.
These differences also intersect with identity — for example, people from multilingual backgrounds may present differently, and girls and women are often underdiagnosed due to stereotypes about who "typically" struggles with literacy.
Co-occurrence is common:
	•	Many people have both dyslexia and dysgraphia, as difficulties with reading and writing often overlap.
	•	ADHD, dyscalculia, and dyspraxia frequently co-occur.
	•	Anxiety or low confidence can develop over time if people are unsupported in school or the workplace.
Strengths often seen in people with dyslexia and dysgraphia include:
	•	Creative thinking and innovation.
	•	Strong oral communication skills.
	•	Big-picture reasoning and connecting ideas.
	•	Visual, spatial, and hands-on problem-solving.
Context and community
The challenges of dyslexia and dysgraphia are often shaped by the environment. In supportive settings — with access to assistive technologies, flexible approaches, and patience around spelling or handwriting — people with these differences can excel. In unsupportive environments, however, barriers may be magnified, leading to frustration or underachievement.
The community of people with dyslexia and dysgraphia is diverse, and language preferences vary. Some prefer "learning differences" rather than "disorders." There is no single profile — so it is important to listen to individuals and avoid assumptions.
Supporting Dyslexia and Dysgraphia in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Provide alternatives to text-heavy communication (audio, visual, or verbal).
	•	Use accessible fonts, clear formatting, and avoid excessive jargon in written materials.
	•	Encourage the use of assistive technology such as speech-to-text or spell-check tools.
	•	Focus on content and ideas, not spelling, grammar, or handwriting style.
	•	Use visual project boards and planners
Dyscalculia and Dyspraxia
What are they?
Like dyslexia and dysgraphia, dyscalculia and dyspraxia are specific learning differences, but instead of primarily affecting reading and writing, they impact numerical processing and motor coordination.
	•	Dyscalculia (sometimes called "maths dyslexia") affects number sense, arithmetic, and mathematical reasoning.
	•	Dyspraxia (also known as Developmental Coordination Disorder, or DCD) affects fine and gross motor coordination, planning, and organisation.
These differences are not linked to intelligence — people with dyscalculia and dyspraxia can thrive academically and professionally when given the right support. Many bring unique strengths such as creative problem-solving, persistence, and new perspectives.
How they are defined
According to the DSM-5:
	•	Dyscalculia falls under Specific Learning Disorder with challenges in maths. It is defined as differences with number concepts, memorisation of arithmetic facts, accurate calculation, and mathematical reasoning.
	•	Dyspraxia (DCD) is defined as challenges in the acquisition and execution of coordinated motor skills, leading to persevered clumsiness, slowness, or inaccuracy that interferes with daily life, learning, and work.
Examples of features include:
Dyscalculia
	•	Challenges with understanding number concepts (e.g., bigger vs. smaller).
	•	Struggling to memorise times tables or arithmetic facts.
	•	Trouble with mental maths or working with quantities.
	•	Difficulty estimating time, money, or measurements.
Dyspraxia
	•	Clumsiness or frequent accidents.
	•	Trouble with tasks requiring fine motor skills (e.g., handwriting, tying shoelaces).
	•	Challenges with planning, sequencing, or organisation.
	•	Fatigue from effortful coordination tasks.
Strengths often seen in people with dyscalculia and dyspraxia include:
	•	Creative and lateral problem-solving
	•	Strong verbal or visual skills
	•	Empathy, persistence, and resilience
	•	Big-picture and imaginative thinking
Context and community
The impact of dyscalculia and dyspraxia is shaped by context. For example, someone with dyscalculia may find traditional maths classes difficult but thrive in applied, hands-on problem-solving, while a person with dyspraxia may find handwriting tiring but excel in oral communication or digital tasks. Supportive environments — such as allowing assistive technology, alternative ways of showing knowledge, or providing extra time — can remove barriers and allow people to demonstrate their strengths. As with all neurodivergence, the community is diverse, and experiences differ widely, so listening to individuals and respecting their preferences is key.
Supporting Dyscalculia and Dyspraxia in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Present numerical information in multiple formats (visual graphs, tables, or verbal explanation).
	•	Avoid putting people on the spot for quick mental calculations.
	•	Offer extra time for planning, organising, and coordination tasks.
	•	Encourage the use of ergonomic equipment like mice and keyboards to reduce strain and dexterity fatigue.
	•	Provide visual and spacial support such as a buddy to help with navigation, or a virtual/ in person tour of the building.

Tourette's and Tic Disorders
What are they?
Tourette's Syndrome and other tic disorders are neurodevelopmental conditions characterised by involuntary movements and sounds known as tics. These are not done on purpose, though they may become more noticeable in stressful situations or when someone is trying hard to suppress them.
Tics can sometimes draw unwanted attention, but with the right understanding and accommodations, people with Tourette's can thrive and contribute unique strengths such as creativity, resilience, and determination.
How they are defined
According to the DSM-5:
	•	Tourette's Syndrome is diagnosed when both multiple motor tics (movements) and at least one vocal tic (sounds) have been present for more than a year, though not necessarily at the same time.
	•	Persistent (Chronic) Motor or Vocal Tic Disorder is diagnosed when a person has either motor or vocal tics (but not both) lasting more than a year.
	•	Provisional Tic Disorder is when tics are present but have lasted less than a year.
Examples of tics include:
	•	Motor tics: eye blinking, shoulder shrugging, facial grimacing, jerking of the head or limbs.
	•	Vocal tics: throat clearing, sniffing, grunting, repeating words or phrases.
Tics often wax and wane, meaning they may be more severe at some times and less noticeable at others.
Supporting Tourettes in the workplace
Important: It's time we move away from requiring diagnosis', towards supporting all employees. It's more important to understand what barriers your employees are facing than to know their personal medical information.
	•	Be patient and avoid drawing unnecessary attention to tics.
	•	Focus on skills, contributions, and performance—not on involuntary behaviours.
	•	Allow flexible breaks if tics become overwhelming or physically tiring.
	•	Promote an open and respectful culture, challenging stigma or misconceptions when they arise.
	•	Avoid interrupting or finishing someone's sentences during a tic episode.

Acquired Brain Injury (ABI)
What is ABI?
An Acquired Brain Injury is an injury to the brain that occurs after birth, rather than being present from birth.
ABI can result from events such as trauma (e.g., head injury), stroke, infection, or lack of oxygen, and it can affect thinking, memory, communication, and behaviour.
Diversity of experience
The impact of ABI varies widely between individuals, depending on the cause, location, and severity of the injury. People may experience:
	•	Memory or attention differences
	•	Slower processing or problem-solving
	•	Communication or speech challenges
	•	Physical changes such as fatigue, coordination, or sensory differences
	•	Emotional or behavioural changes
No two experiences of ABI are exactly the same, so support needs to be personalised.
Supporting ABI in the workplace
Practical ways to support colleagues with ABI include:
	•	Providing clear instructions and written summaries to aid memory
	•	Allowing extra time for tasks and decision-making
	•	Adjusting workload, schedules, and break patterns to manage fatigue
	•	Ensuring assistive technology or memory aids are available
	•	Having open conversations about access needs
	•	Fostering a patient, inclusive, and flexible workplace culture

With the right adjustments, colleagues with ABI can thrive, contribute fully, and bring valuable skills and perspectives to the team.

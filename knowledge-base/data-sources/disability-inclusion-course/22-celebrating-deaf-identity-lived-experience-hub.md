### Video Transcript: Jasper's perspective

Jasper:

Hi, my name is Jasper. This is my sign name. I'm profoundly deaf, autistic, and also live with some chronic illnesses. I use a wheelchair every day and also communicate using British Sign Language and sometimes spoken English too, depending on my health. So for me, the main barriers, the biggest barriers for me are that my health fluctuates very significantly.

So that means what? Some days my health is better compared to other days, which means that one day my health is good, and then the next day it sort of plummets. So my plan for the day, compared to my health, my body and what it allows is very different. And also the other barrier is lots of areas are not accessible for a wheelchair, which means that if I get to a meeting or I have to deliver a presentation or a workshop, I'm very limited in the areas that I can go if the lift is broken.

Breaks, lunch and after work events can be very difficult for me to access because normally at that time the interpreter is either on a break themselves or they have gone home for that day, which means I'm then on my own trying to communicate and understand what people are saying. When I can't hear, the language adjustments that I need are first, flexible and online work, which means that I can pace, second, access to both an interpreter, but also my PA who can sign to me, and last, clear specific information before.

If adjustments are there, colleagues are understanding and compassionate, work can be easy, smooth, and includes everyone. For example, repeating information that a person has said, writing things down and introducing themselves using finger spelling.

### Video Transcript: Rachael's perspective

Rachael:

In terms of my neurodivergence and interviews, honestly for me, it wasn't an issue. I've been masking for a very long time. I'm very old. I used to do a lot of improv and a lot of acting. So for me, the interview was an opportunity to just have that fun, backwards and forwards. I do tend to overexplain.

That's one of my worst traits. I really need to be understood. So, if it's possible to say something in 400 words instead of 5, I'll opt for the 400 words. But that's something I'm starting to understand now is a trait that's related to my neurodivergence. But there's a saying isn't there, when you've met one neurodivergent person, you've met one neurodivergent person, we're all different.

What would've been useful for me, and what I always found useful and when I prepared, I performed the best in interviews, was when they told me what the interview format was gonna be. So, you are gonna do a presentation to the team about the X, Y, and Z. I went through a great interview before I worked for Evenbreak, and they said, right, you can do a presentation. This is what it's about. It's gonna be a panel format. So I went in, I've done all the prep, I've learned all the presentation. I could have done it without the slides, quite frankly. But that was a really great experience for me. And actually what was really brilliant about that organisation was when I didn't get the job, when they gave me the feedback, the feedback was really, really good. So instead of me thinking I haven't got the job because I'm deaf, I didn't realise at the time that I was neurodivergent. I knew that it wasn't because of my disability. I knew the reason that I hadn't been given the job, because I think one of the issues is a lot of disabled people go, well, I've not given me the job because I'm disabled.

And you internalise that and you think, oh, it's because of my disability. Other barriers I've faced when I've been applying for roles is when you go into an interview scenario and the room's already set out and your interviewers might, for example, be sitting in front of a window or there may be a light source behind them.

That makes it really difficult for me to hear. And if it's a panel thing and we've got multiple people talking, perhaps at the same time, I'm kind of doing this backwards and forwards. I'm never afraid to ask for clarification. But sometimes I think other people can find it a little bit embarrassing.

So I think one of the kindest things you can do is in an interview setting when somebody's coming in to say, where's the best place for you to sit? Where would you like to sit? Certainly it affects me as a deafened person, it may well affect other people who have different disabilities. Another barrier, and I know this is really common with women as well as disabled people. We look at a job and if we can't do 100% of the job, we go, oh, there's just no point of me applying for that because I haven't got that one tiny little piece of experience. I think if there's one thing that you could do to get more disabled people to apply for your roles and more women to apply for your roles, is to say please apply for this role even if you haven't got 100% of the experience. Because honestly, it makes such a difference. A lot of the candidates we work with at Evenbreak, they're putting hours and hours into their applications and they will do a cost benefit analysis. Is it worth the 2 or 3 hours of my time if I'm going to be disqualified because I can't do this one particular thing.

So if your job adverts could include, hey, you know what? We hire for attitude. If you can't do 100% of everything, please apply anyway. Please talk to us or just give us a call. Let's have an informal chat because honestly, it makes such a difference. Just talking about small things that make a big difference to me, because of my role, and my role is to make sure that everybody else's adjustments are fair. I don't often speak up about what mine are, and one of the really nicest, kindest things that people can do is to think about my adjustments without me having to ask for them. And this was, it's a small thing, but it meant the world.

We were away at a conference and we needed to find somewhere to eat. And the person that chose the place to eat said, I've chosen this place, we're gonna go early because it'll be quiet so Rach can hear. That meant the world to me. The fact that somebody had considered that. Because normally what happens, you go to a busy restaurant and I just sit there quietly at the end, because I'll be tired anyway, because I've been lip reading most of the day. That meant the world. So small things that are not assumptions that you're just doing because you think, oh, well that person's a wheelchair user, therefore I'm going to put in these adjustments that they haven't asked for.

It's just showing that consideration and that kindness because as I say, I put everybody else first and we all do that, but sometimes it's really nice to have something done that you haven't actually asked for. I've got another colleague, I have psoriasis, which is a skin condition, and if I get a flare up, sometimes it, it can be really quite painful and I'm a little bit flaky. And I've got one colleague who would just sort of come up to me and go, you are a bit flaky. And I go, oh, thank you mate, I'll just have a, you know. It's never done with a, well hey look!, Rach is a bit flaky. They'll just sort of come up really quiet. They've actually got cabbage stuck in your teeth or something like that, and just tells me, and it's just a real kindness and caring. It's not a big song and a dance.

It's just a really small thing that makes a big difference to me, because if I get back to the hotel at the end of the day and I'm covered in bits of skin, I'm gonna be thinking, why didn't anybody tell me that? It's like if you got cabbage in your teeth. Nobody tells you. I mean, I will because I'm gonna be lip reading, I'm going to just very quietly go, you've got cabbage in your teeth. But yeah, it's little things like that that make a real difference.

# Supporting diverse sp

Supporting Diverse Speech
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Language is one of the most powerful tools we have for connection. The way we speak and listen shapes how included—or excluded—people feel. In the context of disability inclusion, diverse speech reflects the reality that communication is not one-size-fits-all. From the words we choose, to the behaviours we model, to the technologies that support us, every interaction can either build a barrier or open a door.
This guide is designed to support individuals and organisations in creating more accessible, respectful, and empowering conversations. By applying the practices talked about in this guide, we can move beyond "good intentions" to meaningful action—creating environments where every voice is heard, valued, and respected.
Practical strategies
	•	Be patient: Don't interrupt, finish sentences, or rush someone. Allow time for them to express themselves.
	•	Normalise pauses: Encourage silence while someone gathers their words. Stay calm and maintain eye contact.
	•	Offer choice in communication methods: Check if the person prefers email, chat, or written follow-ups to supplement verbal conversations.
	•	Be flexible in meetings: Allow extra time for contributions or use structured turn-taking.
	•	Avoid making assumptions: Don't equate speech differences with lack of knowledge.
Inclusive behaviours
	•	Respect autonomy: Ask how someone wants to be supported (e.g., "Would it help if I send the agenda in advance?").
	•	Model inclusion: If someone's speech is interrupted, pause the group and invite them to finish.
	•	Listen actively: Nod, use positive body language, and signal interest while someone is speaking.
	•	Clarify: If you didn't understand, ask politely, e.g., "Could you repeat that last point?" rather than pretending to understand.
	•	Value contributions: Reinforce that everyone's input is as important as anyone else's.
	•	Promote accessibility: Provide transcripts, captions, or note-takers in group settings to reduce pressure on verbal communication.
Assistive tools
	•	Speech-to-text software (e.g., Dragon NaturallySpeaking, Microsoft Dictate).
	•	Communication apps for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC).
	•	Meeting captions (Zoom, Teams, Google Meet built-in).
	•	Text-based platforms (Slack, email, Teams chat) for contributions outside of live conversation.
Lived experience insights
	•	Many people value time and patience above all.
	•	Others highlight the importance of not infantilising them, treat them as the skilled professionals they are, not as people who need pity.
	•	Some prefer direct acknowledgment (e.g., "Thanks for sharing, take your time") rather than pretending the difference isn't there.
	•	Many have highlighted how enough preparation time reduces anxiety. For example, having time to prepare answers to planned questions that they can use to follow when speaking.

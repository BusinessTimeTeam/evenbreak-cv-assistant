### Video transcript: Understanding neurodivergent experiences

Narrator Meghan:

There are lots of terms surrounding neurodiversity that are often used interchangeably. Before we start this module, we wanted to clarify how we interpret and use language. For us, neurodiversity refers to the overarching diversity of our minds, how we think, process, and engage with the world. Our whole society, for example, is a neurodiverse group.

This was a concept coined first by the autistic community in the early to mid 1990s. Later, the concepts of neurotypicality and neurodivergence evolved, with neurotypicality referring to the dominant societal expectation and the term neurodivergent, coined by Kassiane Asasumasu, highlighting the experience of those whose brains and minds function in ways that differ from that dominant societal expectation or ideal of neurotypicality. Within the bounds of neurodivergence, there are many neurotypes or neurominorities, people who share the same divergence.

This includes everything from autism and ADHD to dyslexia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia, bipolar disorder, and sensory processing differences as well. Many others without a diagnostic label also fall within the neurodivergent umbrella. It's important to note that many of these experiences present in different ways for different people and experiences can overlap and intersect.

This is important to understand as it highlights a need for how we think about neurodivergence and support people in our workplace. In this section we'll explore different neurotypes, what neurodivergence really means, and we'll hear from different voices sharing their lived experience in the workplace to help you make support choices that enable everybody to thrive.

### Neurotypes

Neurotypes refer to the different ways human brains function, process information, and experience the world. Just as people have diverse personalities, learning styles, and strengths, they also have unique cognitive profiles.

### Key neurodivergent concepts and language

Understanding neurodivergent language is key to inclusive practice. These terms—often created or reclaimed by neurodivergent people—express identity, validate experiences, and challenge stigma. They help make sense of how people interact with the world, explain needs and boundaries, and describe experiences that are often misunderstood or pathologised in medical or workplace settings.

By recognising this language, we show willingness to listen, adapt, and create spaces for authentic inclusion. Explore the tabs below to learn common terms and why they matter.

Custom Block

Masking

The effort to hide or suppress natural behaviours to fit in. This is often exhausting and can lead to burnout.

Meltdown

A response to extreme overwhelm. It can include emotional outbursts or shutdowns; a sign that someone has exceeded their capacity and may need some time to recharge.

Burnout

A state of exhaustion caused by chronic stress, masking, or lack of support. Neurodivergent burnout can be long-lasting and often misunderstood.

Spiky Skillset

Neurodivergent people may have key strengths in some areas alongside areas of challenge, unlike the more evenly distributed skills expected of neurotypical employees.

Stimming

Short for self-stimulatory behaviour. Movements or sounds that regulate sensory input or express emotions. eg. fidgeting, rocking, tapping, or repeating words.

Special Interests

Deep, passionate interests that bring joy and often lead to expertise. Special interests are a source of strength, not distraction.

Spoon Theory

A metaphor used to describe the limited energy ('spoons') neurodivergent or chronically ill people may have in a day. Tasks require spoons, and running out means needing to rest or recover

Intersectionality

Many neurodivergent people experience multiple overlapping neurodiverse characteristics or identities at the same time.

E.g someone may be autistic and dyslexic.

### Common neurodivergent differences

Neurodivergence is not a single experience. It exists across a spectrum of identities, and even within those identities, individual experiences vary. However, many neurodivergent people share common types of cognitive, emotional, and sensory experiences.

### Summary

Neurodivergent people may share certain common experiences, such as differences in sensory processing, executive function, attention, communication, and emotional regulation, but no two individuals are the same.

These differences are not flaws or deficits, but natural variations in how our brains work. While some of these traits may be misunderstood in traditional workplaces, they are simply alternative ways of processing, interacting, and navigating the world.

Recognising and supporting these differences is essential to building truly inclusive environments.

### Sensory differences

Many neurodivergent people have heightened or reduced sensitivity to sensory input (light, sound, touch, smell, taste).

This can lead to sensory overload or shutdown in environments that feel overwhelming.

### Executive functioning differences

Planning, organising, remembering tasks, and managing time may require more effort. These are not signs of laziness or disinterest, but reflect how some brains are wired to work differently.

### Attention differences

Neurodivergent people may hyperfocus intensely on areas of interest, or have differences&nbsp;with maintaining attention in ways that meet neurotypical expectations, especially in unstimulating or rigid environments.

### Social and communication differences

Preferred styles of communication may differ from typical norms, such as a preference for written communication, direct language, or needing more time to process and respond.

### Emotional regulation and processing

Some may experience differences in emotional intensity, slower recovery from stress, or different expressions of empathy, all of which may be misunderstood in workplaces built around neurotypical expectations.

### Voice hearing, plurality, and altered states

Some neurodivergent people experience internal voices, multiple identities, or altered states of consciousness. These are often misunderstood or pathologised, but can be part of a valid and meaningful way of navigating the world.

Common workplace barriers

Some common barriers include:

- Inflexible schedules or unclear role expectations

- Sensory-unfriendly workspaces (e.g. noise, lighting, open-plan)

- Communication norms that disadvantage directness or non-verbal preferences

- Stigma around emotional expression or mental health

- Inaccessible recruitment and performance review processes

- Limited understanding of masking, burnout, and spikey skillsets

## Image: text aside

A person taking a photo with a camera on a tripod

Shifting the lens

Neurodiversity is not just about conditions or diagnoses, it’s a political and social movement. It reframes difference as a form of diversity to be embraced, not problems to be fixed.

Instead of asking neurodivergent employees to adapt, we must build workplaces that adapt to them.

This includes:

- Designing inclusive systems and environments from the outset

- Valuing different ways of thinking, working, and communicating

- Listening to lived experience, not assuming based on stereotypes

- Moving away from deficit models and towards strength-based support

### Video transcript: applying the social model to leadership

Meghan:

For me as a leader, the social model informs my day-to-day practice and what that looks like is not thinking about the conditions or labels or diagnoses that my team members have, but considering the access needs and barriers that they experience day to day.

So what that looks like in a conversation when a new team member joins my team is:

"Hey, on a Monday morning, we meet for an hour as a whole team to talk about where we're at, as an organisation, that often means that the head of department might give a project update and that I'll come to you to ask you if you want to share anything. Are there any specific barriers that you might experience in that one hour meeting that I can help to accommodate or make adjustments for?"

As opposed to:

"Hey, new team member. What adjustments do you need day to day at work?"

They're really different concepts. One is about being specific and really helping to understand what that person needs then and there to enable them to thrive in every specific situation. But day to day, that's how I use the social model. I use it to inform confident conversations with team members that center around access.

I am very unlikely to know what adjustments all of my colleagues might need day to day, and that's because everyone's adjustments are so different. So it's really important when you're leading to use the social model in a way to help you understand individual barriers and therefore individual access needs.

### Video transcript: top tips for intersectional inclusion

Adam:

I proudly identify as a disabled person, but I know working on a global scale, language and the intersectional facets of human beings impact the use of language and being able to identify in order to get the support and adjustments people need to thrive. So whilst disabled is not a dirty word, we want to understand that one's culture plays a huge part in whether someone wants to identify and feels safe to identify as a disabled person or person with a disability depending on where they are in the world.

Also, things like having no access to diagnosis or information and understanding of different barriers and differences can also stop people getting adjustments put in place, so having an intersectional understanding around disability is really important because it really does impact the language that we use and whether or not people feel safe to get the support.

One thing we can do that will recognise that language is important, we can also start to use narratives, a normalised conversation, simply saying to our colleagues and the people we manage and support, is there anything you need that we can put in place to enable you to thrive in your role? By doing this, we can, they can navigate intersectional barriers and we create a culture where we can normalise this conversation and enable more people to thrive.

### Video transcript: Weekly check-ins

Adam:

Here's a tip to create an inclusive culture.

Why not plan weekly check-ins with our teams just to be sure their wellbeing is supported and they are being able to thrive in the workplace. These check-ins may only take 5 or 10 minutes but having a regular dialogue, regular space that's focused on wellbeing instead of work tasks can really start to create an inclusive culture in the workplace.

### Video transcript: Meeting agendas

Adam:

Here's a top tip for helping create an inclusive culture in our workplace.

For some individuals, not knowing what will be discussed in a meeting can be challenging and create barriers. Why not make sure we are sending meeting agendas around in advance to enable people to think about how they may contribute before the meeting has even started.

### Video transcript: Sharing lived experience

Adam:

Another top tip to help create a more inclusive culture in our workplace. We know that disabled and neurodivergent talent exists throughout all parts of the organisation. We don't always share these stories and representation so therefore I think encouraging your senior leaders to share their lived experience and demonstrate that disabled talent its needed in all parts of our organisation.

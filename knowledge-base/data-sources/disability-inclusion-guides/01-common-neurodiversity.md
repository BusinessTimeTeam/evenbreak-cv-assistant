# Common neurodiversity

Common Neurodiversity Barrier Resources 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Neurodiversity enriches the workplace with different ways of thinking, learning, and problem-solving. At the same time, some colleagues may experience barriers that make everyday tasks—such as writing, speaking, or navigating sensory environments—more challenging. Recognising and addressing these barriers is key to creating spaces where everyone can contribute their strengths.
This guide brings together practical resources for supporting writing, verbal, and sensory barriers, as well as lived experience strategies that highlight what works in practice. It also explores useful physical tools and offers tips for managers to ensure support is consistent, proactive, and empowering.
Supporting writing barriers
For colleagues who experience challenges with written communication, handwriting, or extended written tasks.
Key barriers may include:
	•	Fatigue or frustration from handwriting or typing for long periods
	•	difficulty with spelling, grammar, or sentence structure
Verbal alternatives when possible
	•	Offer the use of voice notes instead of written updates.
	•	Offer to take verbal briefs or summaries in 1:1s.
	•	Use dictation tools (e.g. Dragon, Google Voice Typing, Microsoft Dictate).
Break down written tasks into manageable parts
	•	Provide a checklist for writing tasks (e.g. intro, headings, bullets).
	•	Set clear deadlines for each step (e.g. plan, then draft, then review).
	•	Encourage use of planning tools like mind maps or sticky notes.
Allow extra time for writing tasks
	•	Adjust deadlines for complex writing (especially if proofreading is challenging).
	•	Build in extra time for feedback loops or use of editing tools.
Collaborative writing or paired tasks
	•	Pair with a colleague for co-writing or reviewing.
	•	Employee provides content verbally, another helps structure/write.
	•	Assign writing tasks to others if it's not essential to the role.
Provide access to assistive technology
Free or low-cost tools: 
Grammarly - grammar support and clarity checks. 
Speech-to-text tools - (e.g. built into Microsoft 365, Google Docs). 
Google Keep / OneNote - for quick voice notes and idea capture.
More advanced: 
Read&Write - supports reading, dictation, and word prediction. 
Otter.ai - converts spoken words into written notes. 
Mind mapping apps (e.g. XMind or Miro) – to structure writing ideas visually.
Encourage exploration - the right tool can be a game changer.
Supporting verbal communication barriers
For colleagues who experience challenges with soaking, processing spoken language, or participating in conversations.
Key barriers may include:
	•	Difficulty finding words or expressing ideas verbally
	•	challenges with following fast-paced or complex spoken information
	•	Anxiety or stress in group discussions or presentations
Inclusive verbal communication
	•	Reduce background noise.
	•	Face the person when speaking.
	•	Avoid interrupting or finishing sentences for them.
	•	Check if employees prefer phone calls, video calls, or written communication.
Quick wins
	•	Share notes and recordings.
	•	Provide visual aids alongside verbal instructions.
	•	Encourage employees to use assistive tech (text-to-speech, speech-to- text, note-taking apps and provide training of these).
Practical everyday strategies
	•	Agendas and materials in advance. Give at least 24–48 hours' notice for meetings.
	•	Summaries after conversations. Follow up with an email capturing key decisions, deadlines, or actions.
	•	Structured turn-taking. In group meetings, chair the discussion so everyone has equal chance to speak and knows when their time is coming.
	•	Silence is okay. Allow thinking time, not everyone responds instantly.
	•	Clarify, don't correct. If someone is experiencing delayed word retrieval, listen patiently and ask clarifying questions.
Manager actions
	•	Never assume. Everyone communicates differently. Ask: "What communication methods work best for you?"
	•	Check understanding. Don't just say "is that okay?", instead ask employees to summarise key points in their own words or ask if they would like the information summarised again or with a written follow up.
	•	Avoid rushing. Build in extra time during meetings or feedback conversations.
	•	Use plain, clear language. Avoid jargon and idioms.
	•	Provide options. Allow employees to choose written, verbal, or visual communication methods.
Supporting sensory barriers
For colleagues who experience challenges with noise, lighting, smells, or multiple simultaneous stimuli.
Key barriers may include:
	•	Difficulty focusing in noisy or visually busy spaces.
	•	Headaches or fatigue from bright/fluorescent lights.
	•	Sensory overwhelm in large group settings.
Assistive technology and tools
	•	Noise-cancelling headphones (Bose, Sony WH series).
	•	Light filters for monitors and overhead lights.
	•	White noise or calming sound apps.
Practical strategies
	•	Provide access to quiet, low-stimulation rooms for breaks or focused work.
	•	Offer flexible start/end times and break/lunch times to avoid peak commute or busy office hours.
	•	Allow hybrid/remote working for high-stimulation days.
	•	Promote noise-cancelling headphones in open offices.
	•	Install and maintain dimmable lights or use natural lighting where possible.
	•	Use fragrance-free cleaning products and avoid air fresheners in shared areas.
	•	Limit large group activities in small, enclosed spaces.
	•	Introduce "low-sensory" meeting options (smaller groups, reduced stimuli).
Lived experience strategies
Make tasks visible: 
Notebooks, checklists, post-it notes, whiteboards are often harder to ignore than a reminder buried in a digital app.
Minimise distractions: 
Noise-cancelling headphones, quiet spaces, or hybrid/remote working.
Visual prioritisation: 
Colour-coded task lists or Kanban boards to support clarity and planning.
Body doubling: 
Peer co-working or scheduled working sessions can boost focus and accountability.
Physical tools
They make tasks visible and external 
Challenges often involve holding too much information in your head at once! Writing tasks down offloads that burden and what's visible is easier to remember and prioritise.
Example: 
A post-it note stuck to your monitor is harder to ignore than a reminder buried in a digital app.
They provide visual structure 
Some people often benefit from visual cues to organise their thoughts and actions.
Seeing tasks laid out on a page or wall makes it easier to sequence steps and see progress.
Example: 
Colour-coded post-its can visually separate tasks by urgency or category.
The physical interaction can reinforce focus 
Physically writing something down or moving a sticky note gives a sensory anchor.
This kinaesthetic feedback can be especially grounding for those with attention or sensory regulation differences.
No apps 
Switching between apps or screens can cause distractions or overwhelm.
A notebook on your desk is simple and always accessible.
Sense of completion 
Ticking a box or moving a post-it from "To Do" to "Done" offers instant satisfaction and motivation.

Tips for managers
Have a conversation 
Ask what's challenging and what helps.
Trust different work styles 
Focus on outcomes, not hours.
Break down tasks 
Clarify deadlines and next steps.
Be time-flexible 
Offer flexible hours and focus time.
Use visual/written aids 
Provide written follow-ups and visual tools.
Check in regularly 
Short, structured one-to-ones can help.

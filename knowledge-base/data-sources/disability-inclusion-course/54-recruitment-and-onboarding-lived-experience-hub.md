### Kirsty Fayle - Barriers to employment as a disabled person

Kirsty:

Hi, so my name is Kirsty and about 10 years ago I found out I've got quite a rare condition and it was not something I knew I obviously had. You know, I was quite an independent person and I've had to change my life a lot to adjust to my disability. One of the biggest things is around work.

So I used to actually be an engineer and in full-time work, I now do an office role and only two days a week. It's been really hard trying to navigate the workplace and stuff and trying to find the right work for me. I used to have a career, I even managed to get a full-time office role as well, following my engineering career.

And I was doing all right and stuff, but as my disability was presenting itself more and I was having more symptoms and stuff, I've had to reduce my hours down. And that's something I've really struggled with, because workplaces don't like accommodating that from when you're going from full-time down to like two days a week.

&nbsp;So to try and find work as a disabled person for part-time, you know, it's really rubbish because it's only minimum wage. You know, it's just basic jobs and stuff, and that is hard in itself. When you know that you're capable of doing work, but workplaces aren't willing to allow you just to work such minimal hours and stuff.

That has been hard. Another thing I've found challenging with finding work is when I have ticked that disabled box on the application, I find it's an automatic rejection. I know for a fact that in my application I have ticked every box that they are after, but because I've, you know, I've said I'm disabled, I've not even got an interview, and they're meant to do that.

I've actually found I've had more success not ticking the disabled box, which I shouldn't have to not tick, because then I've got to overcome those hurdles further down and explain why I didn't tick it. I've also found that since having the disability, some employers are really good and you can explain everything to them and they will be understanding, especially when it comes to like, you know, you need to change your day that you're working or for appointments and stuff like that. Or if your symptoms are just really bad that day and you just need to swap that, some will work and will be really understanding, others of them, nah, they really don't. And I found that really challenging, you know, because I like to be honest and I'm very open about my disability and how it affects me.

The other thing I found really hard is I've had to stop driving and I live in a remote place, so I'm limited to the options of where I can get to for work and not only on top of what I can do for work, but I want the option for me to be able to work remotely or hybrid and stuff.

So, you know, if there's a day that I am particularly bad, I can either take the day off or I can work from home and I can kind of manage my condition a bit better. And that's, you know, that's been quite hard but because I work in an office role, having the correct setup is crucial for me in being the best it can be for me to manage my condition and being able to work.

So, you know, I need to, again, be honest with that to make sure I can get that properly set up for me and stuff. It's not easy navigating it as a disabled person, trying to find work and stuff, but we want to work. A lot of us do wanna work, and it's just about finding those diamonds, I guess, and trying to get the right employer for you.

### Matt: Recruitment process with Autism and Dyspraxia

My name is Matt, I am 25 years old and have autism and dyspraxia. I have had various roles in retail and administration since leaving school and I am currently looking for a new role.

It can be very challenging applying for jobs. One of the things I have really struggled with is whether or not to disclose my autism. I have tended to find that when I disclose my autism my application goes no further and when I don’t I have had more success at getting to interview stage. On balance though, in my current application process I have decided to disclose, as by not doing so I am unable to ask for any adjustments during the recruitment process and it is more difficult to succeed in the role.

During the recruitment process itself the interview process can be very challenging. I especially find group interviews problematic. I find it difficult to read body language and to know when to interject in the conversation as I can’t always tell when someone else has finished speaking. It also takes me a little longer to process information so once I’ve formulated my response someone else has usually spoken and the conversation has moved on. It helps me to know in advance if there is some kind of group interview or group exercise so I can prepare as best I can. Another thing which really helps is to have sight of the interview questions in advance so I can prepare properly. Generally having as much information as possible about the format of the interview, who will be interviewing me and the types of questions can make a big difference for me.

When starting a new role it helps to have some time to meet colleagues either in person or online and also to have an opportunity to familiarise myself with the office layout. This makes me feel less anxious, especially if I am able to visit the office before my first day so I know my way around. Whilst I do not need any special equipment, there are a few things which can really help me be successful. As I’ve mentioned I find reading body language and non verbal queues difficult so actually being invited to share my view really helps. I usually have something to contribute; I just need to a little assistance in doing so. Clear instructions and a little extra time to process information also makes a huge difference. I have also had roles where I have had a buddy or someone to check in with and this has been very valuable in helping me to feel supported.

Overall I would like people in the workplace to stop thinking of autism as a disability, we have a unique perspective on the world which can sometimes be challenging for us to deal with but we can also have unique skills which mean that with a little support and adjustments we can make a valuable contribution in the workplace.

### Alex: Insights on barriers

I either get offered a job or interview, then have to turn it down when I find out the job is completely different to what was advertised — something I physically can’t do because of my disabilities. Or I just don’t get interviews at all.

It’s so frustrating. My body doesn’t always work the way I want it to, but my brain does. I know I have so much to offer, but not having worked since 2018 and being disabled seems to hold me back.

The most disheartening thing is when organisations publicly say they’ll guarantee interviews for disabled people who meet the minimum requirements… and then don’t even offer you one. I’ve learned they can say it, not follow through, and it’s still not illegal. It just looks good on paper, I guess.

I wish employers could really see us, disabled people, as a huge, untapped workforce, bringing unique perspectives, skills, and determination. But we need more than good intentions. We need inclusive systems, accessible job descriptions, flexible options, and transparency from the start.

Meg: It's got to be good for business! And Jane, how do you think as an organisation, a company can go about attracting that much more diverse pool of talent?

But I would also, and I'm bound to say this, I know using a niche job board is always going to help because our candidates will say, “if we can see that an employer has paid to advertise their jobs on a job board, that's just for disabled people, that's a really powerful demonstration of their commitment”.

### Video transcript: creating a loud and proud strategy for attracting disabled talent

Adam:

I want to tell you a tip that I think we could all do to help remove one of the biggest barriers to disabled candidates.

For one reason, here at Evenbreak, we know the biggest common barrier that disabled candidates face is not knowing which employer to trust, which employer authentically wants to attract and hire disabled talent and which employer will take their access needs seriously. Now we often see many authentic statements when we look at job ads and job specs, which is great. But these statements often appear right at the bottom of an ad or job spec in small print.

The tip from us is, we need to be proud and loud about wanting disabled talent in our organisations. So why not move back away from small print inclusion and let's have these really key messages right at the top of your job ad and your specs saying that we authentically want disabled talent to apply for roles. We need diversity of thought, and we need diversity of representation right at the top. Let's make sure it's really clear that reasonable adjustments are available, how people can get in contact to discuss any adjustments they might need.

So, let's be loud and proud, and create a truly authentic and anticipatory inclusive welcome.

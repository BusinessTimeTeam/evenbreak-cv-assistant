### Video transcript: text only

Narrator Meghan:

Having a visual impairment, being blind or partially sighted, is about more than a person's level of vision. It shapes how people interact with the world, but it's also about identity, personal lived experience, and the diverse ways individuals navigate daily life and work. Too often blindness is viewed simply as a limitation, but people who are blind or partially sighted bring valuable perspectives, problem solving skills, and great adaptability.

Every individual's experience is different. Some may use screen readers or magnification technologies. Others might rely on tactile information, mobility aids, or sighted guides. The key is recognising that there's no single way to have a visual impairment. Barriers in the workplace often arise not just from the visual difference themselves, but from environments and [00:01:00] practices that assume everyone accesses information visually.

Whether that's an inaccessible document, a poorly designed digital tool, or lack of orientation support in a physical space. According to the World Health Organization, over 2.2 billion people globally experience some form of visual impairment, yet accessible design is still too often treated as an afterthought.

In this section, we'll explore the variety of blind and partially sighted experiences, hearing from lived experience. We'll highlight common workplace barriers and share best practice guidance, covering everything from accessible documents and digital tools to inclusive meeting practices and physical way finding to ensure that colleagues with vision impairments are supported to thrive.

### Navigating the world

Below are some of the most commonly used methods that blind or partially sighted individuals may use to navigate their environment. This is not an exhaustive list - each person has their own preferred techniques and tools.

**Embedded narration script:**

Guide canes
Guide canes are long white canes that help blind people to detect obstacles at ground level, like steps, curbs and changes in terrain.
Guide canes provide tactile feedback about the environment and help the individual move safely and independently.
However, not every blind person will use this method, some may prefer the assistance of a human guide, or a guide dog.

Symbol canes
Symbol canes are shorter, lightweight white canes used by some people with sight loss who have partial or fluctuating vision.
They are primarily used as a visual symbol to let others know the person has a vision impairment and may need extra time, awareness, or support in shared spaces.
Use of a symbol cane is a personal choice and may vary depending on the individual and situation.

Assistive technology
Many blind and partially sighted people use assistive technology to help them navigate their workplace responsibilities.
Assistive technology includes things like screen readers, text to speech software, and magnifiers to help them access the digital world.
These tools remove barriers, enabling blind colleagues to work autonomously and fully contribute their skills and unique perspectives.

Braille.
Braille is a tactile reading and writing system that uses raised dots to replace letters and numbers, allowing reading through touch.
It is an important literary tool, especially for people who are blind from a young age, but many blind people don't read braille today, they use audio or digital technology instead.
Nonetheless, it remains an essential option for equal
access in education and work.

Guide Dogs.
Guide dogs are highly trained assistance animals. They help their handlers navigate obstacles, stop at curbs and steps, and find key locations like doors or crossings.
Beyond mobility, guide dogs also offer companionship and confidence, enabling their handlers to participate more fully in work, travel, and social life.
There is often a long waiting list to receive a guide dog.

## Image: text aside

Common barriers for blind colleagues

- Inaccessible communication - small text sizes, unlabelled images, PDFs that are incompatible with screen-readers etc

- Poorly designed physical spaces - cluttered corridors, visually overwhelming colours/designs, and lack of clear/accessible navigational signage

- Stigma - attitudes surrounding what constitutes blindness (e.g. "you can't be that blind if you can see x")

- Exclusion from visual work - meetings, videos, presentations and training sessions that have no audio or text description

## Image: text aside

Top tips for supporting blind colleagues

- Ensure all documents, presentations and websites are screen-reader compatible

- Learn how to add alt-text and image descriptions to all pictures and diagrams

- Keep workplaces tidy and predictable - don't rearrange things without prior warning

- Remember: clearer communication, easy navigation, and accessible design benefit everyone!

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following statements best reflects the reality of blindness?

Correct answer: D – Blindness and partial sight include a wide range of experiences, from no vision to limited or partial vision such as light or shape perception.

- Answer option: A. All blind people have no sight whatsoever.

- Answer option: B. Blindness always affects central vision.

- Answer option: C. Only people who use a white cane or guide dog are considered blind.

- **Correct answer:** D. Blindness is a spectrum — some people have no sight, while others may see light, colour, or shapes.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following is an example of a barrier that might exclude a blind or partially sighted colleague?

Correct answer: B – Inaccessible communication formats and unlabeled images can prevent colleagues from accessing vital information.

- Answer option: A. Sharing meeting notes in advance in accessible formats

- **Correct answer:** B. Using small text, unlabeled images, or PDFs that aren’t compatible with screen readers.

- Answer option: C. Providing clear, consistent room layouts and signage.

- Answer option: D. Reading out key visual information in meetings.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following actions helps create a more inclusive environment for blind and partially sighted colleagues?

Correct answer: B – Adding alt text and making content screen-reader accessible ensures everyone can engage fully with digital materials.

- Answer option: A. Regularly rearranging office furniture to create a “fresh look.”

- **Correct answer:** B. Adding alternative text (alt text) to images in digital documents and presentations.

- Answer option: C. Using complex, highly visual slides without explanation.

- Answer option: D. Sharing important updates only through posters or infographics.

# The social model

The Social Model
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
The models of disability
A quick comparison
MEDICAL MODEL VS CHARITY MODEL VS SOCIAL MODEL
MEDICAL MODEL
	•	Sees the individual as the problem.
	•	Suggests disability needs to be 'fixed' or cured by a medical professional.
	•	Requires medical diagnostic evidence to provide support.
CHARITY MODEL
	•	Sees the individual as the problem.
	•	Suggests disability is a burden and disabled people are less capable.
	•	Requires medical diagnostic evidence to provide support.
SOCIAL MODEL
	•	Sees barriers in society that prevent access as the problem.
	•	Impairment and disability are not the same. Society must remove barriers.
	•	Empowers disabled individuals by normalising access needs.
Introduction
In a move away from traditional, medicalised frameworks in society that treat disability as an individual 'problem', a 'burden', and a 'drain on society', the social model is a modern framework that shifts the focus away from the individuals' condition or difference, towards the barriers that exist within our world that limit access, participation and equal opportunity.
According to the social model, people are disabled not by their own bodies, minds, or lived experiences, but by external obstacles - whether physical, cultural or attitudinal - that can and should be removed to ensure fair access and inclusion for all. The social model suggests that we have a societal responsibility to remove these barriers, and that in doing so, we can value and celebrate on our differences and diverse strengths, utilising the assets and benefits that come with each individual's unique skills and perceptions.
Fundamental principles of the model
	•	Disability is caused by barriers, not individual experiences
	•	Everyone has the right to equal access and participation
	•	Society has a responsibility to anticipate and prevent barriers, not just react to them when they arise
	•	Disabled and neurodivergent people are their experts in their own experiences, and can share important insights that benefit everyone
	•	Inclusion benefits all of us, by creating more flexible, accessible environments
Understanding and identifying barriers
When we think about removing barriers to access, we often think about physical barriers first, such a staircase that prevents a wheelchair user from gaining access to a building, rather than a ramp or a lift. However, physical barriers are just one kind of barrier, and the next section will give you some examples of how the 5 key barriers may show up in the workplace, as well as the exclusionary impact they can create for your diverse colleagues:
Physical barriers
	•	Examples include narrow entranceways, desks that are too high or too close together, buildings without accessible bathrooms.
	•	These barriers create exclusion by preventing colleagues with mobility differences from moving freely or accessing physical spaces and facilities.
Environmental barriers
	•	Examples include overly noisy working environments, harsh bright lighting, strong fragrances, and a lack of accessible signage to help people navigate around the environment.
	•	These barriers create exclusion by adding to sensory overwhelm and cognitive load for disabled and neurodivergent colleagues, which interferes with their ability to do their work effectively.
Attitudinal barriers
	•	Examples include assuming disabled staff are less capable at performing certain tasks, less likely to participate in work or social events, or seeing adjustments as 'special treatment'
	•	These barriers create exclusion because they create negative assumptions, stereotypes and biases that result in disabled colleagues being left out of key events and passed over for development or promotion opportunities
Systemic barriers
	•	Examples include rigid policies on sick leave, inflexible working hours and locations, and inaccessible practices and processes, which may begin right from the recruitment stage.
	•	These barriers create exclusion because they expect conformity to a 'one size fits all' approach that doesn't work for everyone or take diverse needs into account, and therefore makes it harder for disabled colleagues to get hired or progress within their roles.
Communication barriers
	•	Examples include dense, hard to read written documents or emails, meetings where verbal participation is compulsory, often with no time to prepare in advance, lack of captions and transcripts available for audio and visual content
	•	These barriers create exclusion by preventing colleagues from having access to important information, for example for colleagues with processing differences, partial sight, or deafness, who may need to access information in different formats and medias
Taking action
Removing barriers in the workplace
So, now that you understand the social model principle of identifying barriers in the workplace, and have some examples of how these barriers may show up and cause exclusion for your colleagues, it's time to put together an action plan for how to tackle these barriers in an anticipatory way:
Phase 1: Gathering data and raising awareness
The best way to understand current barriers in your workplace is to speak to colleagues and employees who are most likely to experience them:
	•	Collect employee feedback rough anonymous surveys, focus groups, and 1:1 check in conversations
	•	Consult lived experience by inviting disabled and neurodivergent colleagues and external experts to share their perspectives
	•	Conduct audits on your physical, environmental and digital spaces
	•	Start delivering training and resources to help colleagues identify barriers around them
Phase 2: Analysing and action planning
Once you have built up a picture of the existing barriers in the workplace, it's time to analyse them and create a plan for removing them:
	•	Review all audit data, surveys and feedback forums for recurring barriers
	•	Develop a clear, written accessibility plan alongside your team, including ◦ specific goals (e.g. add captions to all videos within 6 months) ◦ Assigned resources (e.g. individual responsibilities, budgets, and carved time to work towards common inclusion goals)
	•	Prioritise actions that will have the biggest impact across the organisation
Phase 3: Implementing, monitoring, and improving
Once you start implementing your action plans, it's key that every person at every stage takes accountability for their own actions, and effectively monitors the positive change it is having on the workplace:
	•	Begin implementing your new inclusive practices and adjustments
	•	Monitor progress regularly through feedback and reviews
	•	Evaluate the impact against the original timelines and metrics
	•	Be honest about the results with your team as they can help you make amendments along the way
	•	Continue improvements by updating practices as needs change and evolve
Quick wins for leaders
Normalise asking about access needs
Begin team check-ins or one-to-one meetings by asking, "What would make this task/meeting more accessible for you?" This shifts responsibility from the individual having to "disclose" to a proactive team culture of inclusion.
Make information clear and flexible
Share agendas, notes, and action points in advance and follow up in multiple formats (e.g., written, visual, verbal). This reduces communication barriers and helps all team members process information in ways that work best for them.
Build flexibility into work practices
Allow flexibility in working hours, deadlines (where possible), and communication styles. A "one-size-fits-all" approach creates barriers—flexibility empowers employees to work at their best.
Model inclusive language and behaviour
Use respectful, barrier-free language (e.g."disabled people" instead of "the disabled"), and call out inaccessible practices in real time. Demonstrating inclusive habits sets the tone for the team.
Act on feedback quickly
If a team member highlights a barrier—such as overwhelming meetings, inaccessible documents, or noisy environments—take immediate steps to adapt. Quick, visible changes show commitment and build trust.

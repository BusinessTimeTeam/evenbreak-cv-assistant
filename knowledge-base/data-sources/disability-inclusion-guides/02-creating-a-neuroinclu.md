# Creating a neuroinclu

Creating a Neuroinclusive Workplace
A best practice guide from Evenbreak

Introduction
Neurodivergent people bring fresh thinking, creativity, and innovation, all vital to any organisation's success. When their strengths are recognised and supported, they drive powerful change and progress.
Yet traditional workplace norms often create barriers: rigid communication styles, overwhelming environments, unclear expectations, and outdated ideas of "professionalism."
This guide, created by Evenbreak consultants and shaped by lived experience, offers clear, practical steps to build more inclusive workplaces where neurodivergent people can thrive. It's designed to turn awareness into action.

Inside, you'll find
	•	Practical ways to remove barriers and offer support
	•	Tools for inclusive, psychologically safe teams
	•	Guidance rooted in lived experience
	•	Strategies to attract, retain, and develop neurodivergent talent
	•	Real examples of inclusive practices in action
Let's build workplaces where everyone can thrive exactly as they are.

Neurodivergence and common barriers
Neurodiversity describes the natural variation in how people think, process, and interact with the world including Autism, ADHD, Dyslexia, Dyspraxia, and Tourette's. These are not deficits, just different ways of being.
Using the Social Model of Disability, we shift the focus away from what a neurodivergent person can or can't do, and instead, look at what's getting in their way. Often, it's not the neurodivergence itself that creates the challenge, it's the barriers within the workplace.
These barriers can include:
‏	•	Sensory overwhelm from harsh lighting, background noise, or open-plan layouts
	•	Unclear or ambiguous instructions
	•	Fixed work schedules that don't account for individual energy fluctuations or focus patterns
	•	Back-to-back meetings with no recovery time
	•	Communication norms that favour speed over clarity
	•	Misinterpretation of communication differences
Removing these barriers often doesn't require a complete redesign, but it does require intention, listening, and flexibility.

The Business Case
Neurodivergent people make up 15–20% of the global population [1], yet remain significantly underrepresented in the workforce. For example, only around 30% of autistic adults in the UK are in employment [2], meaning businesses are missing out on huge talent and innovation potential.
Neurodivergent employees often bring unique strengths like creative thinking, attention to detail, and strategic insight. Inclusive workplaces benefit from improved problem-solving, innovation, and stronger team performance.
A commitment to neuroinclusion also boosts your employer brand, improves retention, and reduces recruitment costs.
[1] Source: Neurodiversity in Business [2] Source: The Buckland Review of Autism Employment: report and recommendations, February 2024

Building a neuro-inclusive culture
Creating a truly neuroinclusive workplace is about action. To unlock the strengths of neurodivergent employees, organisations must actively work to remove societal barriers, embed inclusive behaviours, and ensure psychological safety at every level.
Below are practical actions you can take to make that a reality.

Make communication accessible for different minds
Neurodivergent employees may process and retain information differently.
How to do it:
	•	Follow up verbal instructions or meetings with written bullet-point summaries
	•	Be direct; avoid idioms or vague language
	•	Use visual aids (e.g. flowcharts, Kanban boards)
	•	Give time to process; allow written follow-ups instead of on-the-spot responses
Top Tip: Create a team communication preferences agreement and make it visible.

Foster psychological safety
Employees must feel safe to share needs without fear of judgment.
How to do it:
	•	Ask open questions: "What helps you do your best work?"
	•	Don't assume: ask, listen, and respond supportively
	•	Don't minimise challenges ("push through" or "you got this!" Is not helpful)
	•	Model openness by sharing your own challenges or support needs
Top Tip: Make psychological safety a part of leadership training.

Make recognition inclusive
Traditional recognition often overlooks less visible strengths.
How to do it:
	•	Offer recognition in different ways: private messages, newsletters, anonymous feedback
	•	Celebrate effort and creativity, not just speed or visibility
	•	Ensure quieter contributors aren't overlooked
Top Tip: Ask how employees prefer to be recognised, public praise isn't for everyone.
Train everyone
Consistent, mandatory training signals that inclusion is a shared responsibility
How to do it:
	•	Make neurodiversity training mandatory for all levels
	•	Ensure consistent language and expectations
	•	Use real-life scenarios and practical examples
	•	Define inclusive behaviour clearly
	•	Involve neurodivergent employees in designing/delivering training
Top Tip: Refresh training regularly; include it in onboarding and performance reviews.

Embed inclusive practices in daily operations
Inclusion should be a standard part of the everyday way that things work, rather than individual employees having to ask for help.
How to do it:
	•	Normalise flexibility: remote work, flexible hours, meeting-free days
	•	Run inclusive meetings: share agendas early, allow written input, provide summaries
	•	Use plain language in policies and break info into chunks
Top Tip: Audit recruitment, onboarding, and performance systems with a neuroinclusive lens.
These changes aren't just supportive, they're essential for building workplaces where neurodivergent employees can thrive.


Intersectional inclusion
Neurodivergent employees don't exist in a vacuum, they may also face racism, sexism, ableism, homophobia, transphobia, or classism. These overlapping identities shape their experiences and the barriers they face at work.
Ignoring intersectionality means inclusion efforts may leave people behind, especially those from marginalised groups.
Below are practical actions you can take to make that a reality.
Common barriers
	•	Delayed or missed diagnoses in ethnic minority communities
	•	Social stigma related to both neurodivergence and identity
	•	Greater risk of workplace discrimination

What inclusive practice looks like
	•	Don't assume lived experience is the same. A white autistic employee and a queer South Asian autistic employee will likely face different barriers.
	•	Collect intersectional data, break down engagement or feedback by multiple identities.
	•	Elevate diverse voices, ensure panels, resources, and stories reflect a range of identities.
	•	Use inclusive training, show how race, gender, and neurodivergence interact in real life.
	•	Check representation, avoid stereotypes in language and imagery.

Practical actions
	•	Review policies with intersectionality in mind.
	•	Offer culturally competent mental health support.
	•	Acknowledge cultural and religious holidays in comms and calendars.
	•	Build flexible systems for both cultural and neurodivergent needs.
	•	Run listening sessions for neurodivergent staff from marginalised groups.
	•	Provide mentors who understand both neurodivergence and systemic bias.
Intersectional inclusion means valuing all parts of someone's identity, not just one.

Neuroinclusive recruitment
Inclusive recruitment is essential for attracting, selecting, and retaining neurodivergent talent. It involves a proactive approach at every recruitment stage, from the initial job posting through to long-term career development, to remove barriers and promote equity.
Neuroinclusive recruitment also signals that your organisation genuinely values cognitive diversity and creates equitable pathways for success.

Vacancy announcements
	•	Use plain, jargon-free language in job descriptions. Avoid unnecessary corporate buzzwords, acronyms, or vague terms like "must be a people person."
	•	Be specific about essential vs. desirable criteria, and limit requirements to what's genuinely necessary.
	•	State your inclusive values explicitly. Include statements such as: "We welcome applications from neurodivergent candidates and are committed to providing an inclusive, accessible recruitment experience. Adjustments are available at any stage."
	•	List job postings on inclusive platforms like Evenbreak, and share roles via neurodivergent advocacy groups and community networks.

Candidate attraction 
	•	Partner with neurodiversity-focused networks and organisations to connect with diverse candidates.
	•	Attend or sponsor inclusive job fairs and neurodivergent-led career events.
	•	Create candidate-facing content that demonstrates neuroinclusion, e.g., "Meet our neurodivergent team members" spotlights or blog posts on inclusive workplace culture.
	•	Audit your employer brand and careers page for accessibility and inclusivity, including video captions, screen-reader compatibility, and plain language explanations.

Application Process
	•	Offer alternative formats for submitting applications (e.g., video, bullet-point summaries, portfolio submissions, creative presentations).
	•	Remove unnecessary requests, like exact employment dates or lengthy narrative CVs.
	•	Allow candidates to indicate any support or adjustments needed during the process via a simple, welcoming prompt (e.g., "Is there anything we can do to make the process more accessible for you?"). Include some examples of adjustments you have provided for past candidates.

Interview Stage
	•	Send interview questions and an agenda in advance. This gives candidates time to process and prepare.
	•	Offer alternatives to traditional interviews, such as work-based trials, take-home tasks or role-specific simulations or shadowing.
	•	Train all interviewers in neuroinclusive recruitment, including how to avoid bias based on communication style or eye contact, for example.
Offer the following:
	•	Remote or quiet interview spaces
	•	Allowing for camera-off options during video calls
	•	Scheduling interviews at times that suit the candidate's energy rhythms or other responsibilities, e.g. medication times, PA support, childcare etc.

Onboarding and career progression
Starting a new job can be particularly stressful for neurodivergent individuals due to uncertainties around expectations, workplace culture, and social dynamics.
A well-planned induction can significantly reduce this stress, enhancing confidence, comfort, and productivity from day one.
Onboarding strategies:
	•	Create a visual onboarding roadmap with key tasks, people, and timelines.
	•	Assign a buddy or mentor to support the first 3 months.
	•	Break induction material into chunks and allow self-paced learning.
	•	Set clear expectations on role, communication norms, and success measures.
	•	Hold regular check-ins early on to address challenges quickly.

Career progression
Publish clear progression frameworks including how and when promotions, raises, or development opportunities occur.

Offer multiple formats for training and development, such as: 
	•	Written guides 
	•	Recorded tutorials 
	•	1:1 coaching or mentoring
	•	Regular check-ins with neurodivergent employees to identify potential barriers and co-create tailored development plans
	•	Celebrate and share stories of neurodivergent success in your organisation to boost visibility and representation

### Mary: advocating in the film industry

Working in film and TV as a disabled person can be really challenging, especially when you’re freelance. Most of the time, I'm on a daily or weekly contract, so there’s an anxiety of the job stopping at any moment. This makes it harder to ask for any support, even something as small as needing a seat, as there's the fear that you'll loose your job. You’re always aware that there’s someone else who can physically do more, and you don’t want to risk losing work, especially if your on a high budget production, where the competition is strong.

The hours can be another huge challenge. In some departments, like the shooting crew, you can be contracted for 12 hours but only work 10 or 11. That means you can’t say no if they need you for that extra hour or two. And if they run over that 12 hour window it's difficult to say no to extra hours, because things just have to get done, you're letting the team down and giving them more work to do. There’s this unspoken expectation to push through, no matter how exhausted you are. It's hard for perfectly healthy and fit people to keep up let alone if you have a disability.

Construction departments have actually made progress thanks to BECTU, with better hours and rates. But if you’re doing similar work in props for example, you might still be working a minimum of 10 hours a day – often for less pay. The work itself can be just as physically demanding, but the support and protections aren’t equal.

I’ve definitely have to hide my struggles to keep my job safe. If I’m late, I’ll say there was traffic or my car wouldn’t start, because saying “I was so exhausted I couldn’t get out of bed” doesn’t feel acceptable. Especially when the working conditions are not great for everyone and your boss is working even harder and longer hours and seems to be coping fine – it makes you feel like you don’t have a valid reason to struggle.

A lot of this comes down to who your boss is. Some people are really understanding and will try to accommodate you, but others simply don’t have the time or capacity to. There isn’t a consistent approach, so it’s always a bit of a gamble.

I am very lucky to have a manageable condition that enabled me to continue working in my dream job. But there is no chance I would be able to continue working if I was in a wheelchair or needed an interpreter for example. There is no time for anyone to think about extra steps to accommodate needs when there's a billion other things to do. The pace of film/TV is insanely fast (the day to day work but also the turnaround of productions) and the hours are so long that there is no space to be inclusive.

Groups like TripleC and the Disabled Artists Network Community are trying to make a difference – they’ve been creating guidelines and offering support. But there’s still not enough awareness, and the industry as a whole has a long way to go in making accessibility a normal, expected part of working life.

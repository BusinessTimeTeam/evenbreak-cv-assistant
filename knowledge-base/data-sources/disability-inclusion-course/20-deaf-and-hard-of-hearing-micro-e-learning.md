### Video transcript: introduction to deaf identity

Narrator Meghan:

Being deaf or hard of hearing is about more than just your hearing level. It's also about culture, identity, and community. Deaf identity can be deeply connected to shared language such as New Zealand Sign Language, British Sign Language, or American Sign Language, as well as to the unique experiences and traditions of the deaf community.

Too often workplaces view deafness only through the lens of hearing loss or as a medical condition to be fixed, which overlooks the pride, belonging, and richness of deaf culture. It's also important to note that not every deaf or hard of hearing person will have the same experience. Some may primarily use spoken language, others may use sign language, and many will use a combination depending on the context.

This shapes different experiences within the workplace. In the workplace, barriers can arise when assumptions are made about how someone communicates or when accessibility options are missing, such as captioning, sign language interpreters, or hosting a meeting in a quiet space. According to the World Health Organization, over 5% of the global population, which is more than 430 million people, experience barriers related to hearing.

Yet many workplaces remain unprepared to meet those differing communication needs. In this section we'll explore deaf identity in its many forms. Highlight common barriers and stereotypes, and share best practice approaches such as accessible communication options, inclusive meeting design, and recognition of deaf culture, which can ensure deaf and hard of hearing colleagues are fully included and able to contribute their skills and talents to the workplace.

### Inclusive communication:

## Multimedia: audio

Optional voiceover

Audio: `193aec98-7e7b-404f-9af9-bf.mp3` (52 seconds; present)

**Embedded narration script:**

Do's
Use phrases like Deaf, Deafened (or late deafened), Hard of hearing and Non-aural
Get a deaf person's attention before starting to talk, by waving in their line of sight
Speak one at a time when in large groups to help with lip reading or interpretation
Keep communication brief and to the point, using a variety of methods including writing and visual aids

Don'ts
Phrases like 'falling on deaf ears' or 'the silence is deafening', Hearing impaired, Deaf-mute and Deaf and dumb
Raise your voice, overly annunciate, or speak really slowly as this changes the shape of your lips and impacts lip reading
Talk to the interpreter instead of the deaf person
Don't be afraid to rephrase or repeat yourself
Assume communication ability - not every deaf person signs

### Barriers for deaf colleagues

In this section, you’ll explore common workplace barriers that Deaf and hard of hearing colleagues may experience — and practical ways to remove them. These examples show how small, intentional actions, communication choices, and inclusive design decisions can create equitable access and a more inclusive working environment for everyone.

## Multimedia: audio

Optional voiceover

Audio: `9eb309c3-1415-4d2b-affe-a2.mp3` (80 seconds; present)

**Embedded narration script:**

Workplace barriers
Inaccessible job applications and lack of tailored onboarding processes to help deaf colleagues acclimatise
Deaf colleagues often miss out on office social conversations and important updates because interpreters aren't present
Lack of reasonable adjustments like extended meeting lengths to allow interpretation time, and materials in advance
Noisy environments that interfere with hearing aids, and poor lighting preventing lip reading
Lack of understanding of deaf culture, and fatigue from communication barriers creates mental health impacts

Solutions
Create accessible recruitment processes, and train managers to ensure appropriate deaf-specific onboarding needs are met
Provide basic sign language training for all staff, and ensure important updates or news are shared in note form
Encourage open discussions about access needs, and adjust events and meetings to account for translation from sign language
Create adaptive, flexible workspaces, with reduced noise level and clear lights, or work from home options
Create networks for deaf colleagues to connect and celebrate deaf culture, and provide inclusive mental health support

Top tip: Find out more about barriers for deaf colleagues and how to remove them in our 'understanding deaf identity' guide.

## Image: text aside

Illustration of two people celebrating

Top tips for inclusive communication with deaf colleagues

- Check in - ask your colleagues what support they need and create a culture where feedback is welcomed.

- Accommodate deaf people's support systems - this may include t-loops for hearing aids, an interpreter or&nbsp;personal assistant, or a hearing dog.

- Plan in advance - interpreters can take up to 6 weeks to book for an events, so check access needs and communication preferences early.

- Build in extra time - time for the deaf colleague and interpreters to get to know each other before events, time for translation between sign and English, time for breaks.

- Learn some basic sign language to involve deaf colleagues in conversations, and send written follow ups after meetings and quick chats to ensure no one misses out.

### Which of the following best describes deaf identity?

Correct answer: C – Deaf identity encompasses a broad spectrum of experiences and is rooted in community, culture, and shared expression — not just hearing level.

- Answer option: A. A medical condition that limits someone’s ability to hear and must be “fixed.”

- Answer option: B. Only people who are completely deaf and use sign language.

- **Correct answer:** C. A rich and diverse community that includes people with different levels of hearing and celebrates Deaf culture, language, and expression.

- Answer option: D. Anyone who occasionally experiences mild hearing loss.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following is not an inclusive communication practice when speaking with a deaf colleague?

Correct answer: D – Over-enunciating or shouting distorts lip shapes and makes lip reading harder — clear, calm speech is best.

- Answer option: A. Speaking clearly, one person at a time, and maintaining visibility for lip reading.

- Answer option: B. Getting the person’s attention by waving in their line of sight before talking.

- Answer option: C. Following up verbal conversations with notes or visual aids.

- **Correct answer:** D. Raising your voice and over-enunciating to make your lips easier to see.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following workplace actions helps to create a more inclusive environment for deaf colleagues?

Correct answer: A – Planning ahead, checking preferences, and providing interpreters or materials in advance ensures equitable access and participation.

- **Correct answer:** A. Checking preferences, booking interpreters and sharing materials in advance to support full participation.

- Answer option: B. Assuming every deaf person uses sign language and providing an interpreter without asking.

- Answer option: C. Avoiding discussions about deaf culture to prevent discomfort.

- Answer option: D. Expecting deaf employees to adapt to standard meeting formats.

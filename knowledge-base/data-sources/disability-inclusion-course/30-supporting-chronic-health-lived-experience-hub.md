### Video transcript: Jane's lived experience

Jane Hatton:

Hi, I'm Jane from Evenbreak. I'm the founder and CEO. I'm a white woman in her mid- sixties with short blonde hair and a teal jacket and a navy and white dress. I became disabled in my forties. I'm one of the 83% of disabled people who become disabled as adults. And I had a chronic spinal condition, which required, uh, numerous surgeries on my lower spine and my neck.

One of which meant that I actually ended up, um, unable to walk and with very little ability to sit or stand. And so I spent about seven years lying flat in bed. So that was the first, uh, social model, experience of disability, and what I discovered was that there were things I could do, even lying flat in bed, with the right adjustments.

So I had a laptop suspended above me, which meant that I could still work, I could still use a phone. This was before the pandemic, so there weren't many Teams meetings and Zoom meetings and things like that, but maybe I was ahead of the curve in using that kind of way of communicating and doing business.

As far as the medical model was concerned, I relied on medication to manage the pain, but actually being able to live my life was very much around the social model and what adjustments could I make. Thankfully, over many years I became much more mobile, being able to walk just a little bit further, stand for just a little bit longer and sit for just a little bit longer.

And so now I'm most days and with medication as mobile as most people, although I do have chronic pain, so my access needs have changed very much. I no longer, most of the time, need to lie flat with a laptop suspended above me. But I do have chronic pain, which means that I have to constantly balance the effects of the pain with the side effects of medication.

And so that might change almost hour by hour. On a bad pain day, I have to make the decision if I take enough medication to manage the pain, I'm not going to be able to think straight in order to do my job.

So there's a constant juggling of medication versus pain. And in terms of adjustments, what that means is that I might work at different times of the day to other people.

It might be that in a morning I'm not very productive because I'm waiting for the pain medication to kick in, and I might be really productive in the evening. And so my hours are very untypical. And unpredictable.

So it may be that I work weekends or evenings because that's when the pain levels are manageable, or it might be that I work at different times, and so my adjustment needs have very much changed over the years, and my team understand that.

### Alice: Living with undiagnosed chronic pain

I was born with a rare anomaly called Pyloric Atresia, and I have lived with chronic pain ever since. I've never really known if the pain was directly related, or due to some other experience or complication, but years of tests and being unable to get a concrete diagnosis left me questioning the validity of my own experiences.

After more than 15 years battling the medical system, I fell into the trap of masking my symptoms, and hid the constant anxiety and stress that came with expectations in physically demanding roles.

That was until I became an inclusion specialist and I was lucky enough to meet people who never asked&nbsp;for 'proof' or a medical certificate, who simply&nbsp;provided me with empathy, care and understanding, no questions asked. I was lucky enough to find an inclusive&nbsp;workplace and an amazing manager who encouraged me to step back when I was experiencing painful flares, to prioritise my wellbeing and to be open and honest about my experiences, because there's strength in modelling vulnerability.

Sometimes I still struggle with identity, and how to define myself within the disabled community, I guess I still have a little bit of imposter syndrome despite fully believing in the social model, and the right that every person has to true belonging and acceptance, regardless of medical status.

But now, I'm proud to work in a role every day where I get to walk in the footsteps of amazing advocates and allies who came before me, and hopefully make it that little bit easier for the next person in line whose lived experiences&nbsp;have ever been questioned or misunderstood, to feel that little bit safer to be who they are. &nbsp;&nbsp;

### Video transcript: Naomi's lived experience

Naomi:

Hello, I'm Naomi, and here is my lived experience with ME/CFS with some brief description, barriers and adjustments that can be made in the workplace. First, brief description, ME/CFS stands for myalgic encephalomyelitis, or chronic fatigue syndrome, which is a complex long-term condition affecting multiple systems.

Some of the symptoms include debilitating fatigue, post exertional malaise, cognitive issues, sleep disturbances, pain and dizziness, plus a fair few more. Basically, if your body regulates it, we can have a lot of trouble with it, and this includes digestion and temperature control.

Some of the barriers for working I personally have found is transportation. I find it much better to do remote work.

Also, my fluctuating symptoms, as my energy levels and pain levels can affect me differently day to day. Also, with some people, there's an extreme lack of understanding. I've been very fortunate in that a lot of the lack of understanding I get, it comes from almost toxic positivity rather than negativity, but it can still have a bit of an impact. And what I mean by that is they're very sympathetic and they go, "I know", when they don't. This can be sometimes not great. It's better than the negative stuff of "I don't believe you", but it's not great.

Some of the reasonable adjustments that can be made for this are flexible working hours or part-time roles, remote or hybrid working options, regular rest breaks, quiet spaces, some assisted technology or ergonomic equipment. Have some outcome-based performance measures and phased return to work or even job sharing.

I've personally found all of these, most of these, perfect. I don't do hybrid because I don't drive, and we have no major office to go to at the moment. But other things I've found is having pajama days and work days being the same. Luckily I'm not people facing most of the time, so it's not too much of a problem.

But yeah, there will be days, especially in winter where I'm either working from bed or in my pajamas, or certainly in a row keeping very warm and cozy, and that can really make a big difference. As well as having the support from my team who are amazing and understanding when it comes to me being a bit brain foggy, sometimes I can just zone out and they're like, "okay, let's, let's give this particular target or job a bit of a break and we can come back to it."

Also, having a lot of autonomy and control over the workload and schedule is amazing. Just being able to prioritise the jobs you know you can do that day and being able to set aside jobs you know can wait. What would also be good would be access to occupational health advice, having clear communication and then being encouraged to pace yourself properly, because again, going too fast or for that matter, going too slow, can be really detrimental.

Again, it's finding that perfect balance, which for us, is a bit of a narrow margin unfortunately, but for the most part we're perfectly capable. We're bright, we try to be incredibly productive.

The fact that we can't quite achieve that physically is unfortunate, but we are very productive. We are very intelligent, as anyone else.

### Video transcript: What inclusive language means to me

Rox:

Hi, I'm Rox, and in this video I'm here to talk to you about language. Inclusive language, at its core, at its heart is about respect. It's about choosing words that don't unnecessarily create barriers, reinforce stereotypes, or even reduce individuals down to list of labels or medical conditions. Language is one of our most powerful tools when it comes to creating an inclusive environment, but it's only as powerful as the confidence of the person using it.

So sometimes I imagine it's like being asked to pick up a chainsaw. Technically anyone can do it, but people focus on the amount of power and therefore risk that comes with it and feel woefully unqualified, and we'll avoid it. And in that avoidance, in that silence, are we actually doing more harm than good?

Probably as a kid growing up in the nineties, people, particularly adults, would do back flips to avoid acknowledging my disability, discussing my disability, and they definitely didn't even want me calling myself disabled, they would say, Rox, you are not disabled. You are not unable. You can do whatever anyone else can do. And ultimately, as a cocky, competent kid who thought, right, I'm gonna do that, and therefore couldn't do everything because they faced barriers and couldn't have a conversation around it, I internalised that. I felt like I was a failure because I've been told I could do anything, when in fact I couldn't.

And that's honestly still something I'm unpicking today, but what's really helped is being able to reclaim that disabled label, which probably brings me onto a question that you may have. A lot of people have asked, Rox, what's the right language use? Is the disabled person or person with a disability?

Well, as you might tell from my story, I prefer the term disabled person. I see myself as disabled under the social model of disability. I'm disabled by barriers in society, not a condition that I bring to the space or society. It also helps me see myself as disabled under the rights model of disability that I am a protected characteristic under that disabled label, and I have rights and protections I can fight for. So for me, disabled person is my preference. However, I want to acknowledge that a lot of people still prefer, rightfully so, the term person with disability because that acknowledges them as a person first, and means that their experiences aren't led by their disablement.

My biggest tip to give you is, apart from think when you need to label it, because actually when do you need to call someone disabled or with a disability really, is, if you actually do, ask them, mirror their language that they want to use as long as it's not derogatory or harmful, ultimately suitable for work, go ahead. Mirror that language. Now, I hope this has helped you start to feel a bit more confident around language use, but I just want to leave you with a few tips.

Language is a personal journey, so always reflect and mirror other people's language where you can. Never assume, just because you watched this video with me and I've said my preference is disabled person or you know someone that has similar or comparable lived experience of the person you are going to speak to, don't assume that they'll want the same.

Whilst there's kind of no right or wrong answer, we would say try and avoid terms like suffers from, afflicted by or wheelchair bound. For example, wheelchair or a wheelchair is something that's really supportive to me. It's all for independence, not something I'm stuck with. So try and use terminology like lives with, experiences and wheelchair user. Sorry to tell you, but to err is human, we are going to make mistakes. Even I will make mistakes in regards to language.

It's not about never making the mistakes, obviously trying not to, but it's about being able to reflect and step back. So if you do make a mistake, don't overly correct and apologise and put a disabled person or person with a disability in the space of having to make you feel better or reassure you that it's okay, just correct, move on, and reflect later. Now I hope this helps, and thank you for listening.

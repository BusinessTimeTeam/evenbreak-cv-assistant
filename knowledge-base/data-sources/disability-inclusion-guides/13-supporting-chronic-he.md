# Supporting chronic he

Supporting Chronic Health in the Workplace 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak 
Introduction 
Non-visible disabilities make up an overwhelming percentage of disabled experiences, with around 75-80% of all disability being neurological or physiological experiences that can’t immediately be seen by others. 
This goes to show that we really can’t judge our colleagues or their experiences just by looking at them, and we don’t understand the internal challenges and difficulties others may be facing, unless we ask them supportive and non- intrusive questions like ‘How are you finding X? Is there anything I can do to make your role a little easier or more accessible for you?’. 
Long term health experience is an umbrella term that covers a wide variety of conditions and circumstances, and each experience will affect people in different ways, including that there may be good days and bad days when it comes to health, as chronic illnesses and injuries can fluctuate over time. Common examples include: 
Chronic pain and fatigue – conditions like fibromyalgia, arthritis, or chronic fatigue syndrome can make standing, sitting, or concentrating for long periods difficult. 
Endometriosis – can cause debilitating pain, fatigue, and require time off for medical procedures. 
Cancer - can create nausea, pain, brain fog and need for intensive medical treatments 
Diabetes - which can cause dizziness, lethargy, and medications that are required to be taken at very specific times of the day 
Long COVID – may result in ongoing fatigue, brain fog, breathlessness, or other systemic symptoms. 
Other chronic illnesses – including autoimmune conditions, or heart disease, which may require regular monitoring, medication, or flexible working arrangements. 
Injuries that cause severe pain and discomfort, loss of feeling in targeted areas of the body, potentially missing limbs and phantom nerve pain etc. 
These conditions are often non-visible, meaning colleagues may not appear to need support, which can increase the fear and reluctance for certain individuals to ask for adjustments or help, for concern around disclosure and sharing of personal medical information, which is often required as ‘evidence’ to provide vital access requirements. 
Addressing the stigma 
Unfortunately, chronic health experiences are one of the largest areas of discriminatory attitudes and stigmas in our society. Employees with chronic health conditions often encounter misconceptions and derogatory attitudes about the ‘validity’ of their lived experiences. 
Many chronic health experiences can be rare and difficult to categorise and medically diagnose (such as Ehlers Danlos Syndrome, Postural Orthopedic Tachycardia Syndrome, and many others) which means that there are extremely long and difficult processes to go through in the medical system to receive official diagnosis. 
Despite this many workplaces and sectors of our society in general still require medical ‘evidence’ and official diagnosis in order to provide support, and this medical model framework leads to negative attitudes that create exclusion and discrimination: 
	•	"You don't look sick." This assumption invalidates the experiences of individuals whose conditions are not outwardly visible, leading to a lack of understanding and support. 
	•	"You're too young to be in pain." Age-related biases can result in younger employees being dismissed when reporting chronic pain or fatigue. 
	•	"I saw you walk yesterday; you clearly don't need a wheelchair." Such comments overlook the fluctuating nature of many chronic conditions, where symptoms can vary daily. 
	•	"Just push through it." This advice can be harmful, ignoring the need for appropriate accommodations and self-care. 
	•	"It's all in your head." Dismissing physical symptoms as psychological can prevent individuals from receiving necessary medical attention and support. 
These biases contribute to a culture of disbelief and marginalisation, making it challenging for employees to seek help or disclose their conditions. 

The impact of stigma 
Stigma related to chronic health conditions in the workplace can have profound effects on employees' well- being and career progression. Research indicates that: 
Missed opportunities: 
Approximately one-third (33%) of employees with chronic health conditions report missing out on opportunities for more hours or projects due to their condition. Additionally, 25% have missed out on promotion opportunities, and 21% have received negative feedback or bad reviews because of their chronic health conditions. Harvard Chan School of Public Health 
Emotional impact: 
Over 40% of employees with chronic health conditions have felt guilty, and nearly 40% have felt embarrassed about their health conditions at work. (Harvard Chan School of Public Health). 
These statistics highlight the significant impact stigma can have on employees with chronic health experiences, affecting their job satisfaction, performance, and overall mental health. 
Common barriers 
Physical environment barriers – lack of ergonomic seating, adjustable desks, or spaces to rest when fatigued can exacerbate experiences of pain, and prevent full concentration on tasks and team collaboration 
Rigid schedules and workloads – fixed hours or inflexible deadlines can make managing symptoms difficult, for example if some has to take medications at certain times of the day, or finds they experience extra brain-fog or flare ups early in the morning so they struggle with their 8am shift. 
Inaccessible policies – sick leave or performance review processes that don’t account for flare-ups, surgeries, or medical appointments, for example things like attendance policies, limited paid sick days etc. 
Limited access to career development opportunities – Chronic health conditions may lead to missed training sessions, networking events, or promotions if workplaces do not proactively offer flexible alternatives, resulting in slower career progression and professional isolation.
Best practice tips 
for supporting chronic health experiences 
Offer flexible working arrangements – allow remote work, flexible hours, or rest breaks to accommodate fluctuating symptoms. 
Work together as a team to delegate workload – prioritise tasks, share deadlines in advance, and consider redistributing work during flare-ups to support each other. 
Utilise Assistive technology – integrate team and individual software or tools that reduce strain, like dictation software, screen readers, or task planning apps. 
Categorise tasks - if a member of the team is experiencing strain or a flare up, they might find some tasks easier to complete than others. Work together to categorise tasks as ‘high intensity’ like delivering training that requires a lot of energy and engagement, vs ‘low intensity’ which might be putting together a simple report or presentation, and then work these around flare-ups or flexible working hours. 
Have regular check-in’s and open communication - share your experiences and needs with each other, and create a team culture where discussing accessibility is encouraged. 
Encourage asynchronous engagement - record meetings, training events and other live interactions so that colleagues with chronic health experiences who may be unable to attend due to pain management or medical appointments are able to catch up in their own time and don’t feel excluded. 

Quick wins for leaders 
To address and mitigate stigma in the workplace, organisations can implement several strategies: 
Education and Awareness: Provide training sessions to educate employees and leadership about chronic health conditions, their impact, and the importance of empathy and support. 
Encourage Open Dialogue: Create safe spaces where employees feel comfortable discussing their health conditions without fear of judgment or discrimination. 
Implement Clear Policies:  Develop and communicate policies that support employees with chronic health conditions, including flexible working arrangements and accommodations. 
Promote Allyship: Encourage colleagues to support one another, fostering an environment of understanding and inclusivity. 
Lead by Example: Management should model inclusive behaviours and attitudes, setting the tone for the rest of the organization, and sharing examples of their own experiences and adjustments. 
By actively addressing stigma and promoting inclusivity, workplaces can become more supportive environments for employees with chronic health conditions, leading to improved job satisfaction and productivity.

# Inclusive recruitment

Inclusive Recruitment 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Creating a truly inclusive workplace starts with recognising and valuing the diverse talents, perspectives, and potential of all individuals. Disability inclusion is not just about compliance; it's about building a culture that empowers everyone to thrive.
This best practice guide is designed to support employers, HR professionals, and hiring managers in developing more inclusive recruitment and onboarding processes. Whether you're just starting your inclusion journey or looking to refine existing practices, this guide offers practical steps and insights to help you attract, support, and retain disabled talent.
Hiring for talent and potential
Rather than specific experience or qualifications
Identifying the best person for the job is about future potential, not previous privilege. Hiring someone because they have sector experience, or a specific degree does not guarantee their performance. Transferable skills can be very valuable. Skills-based recruitment is becoming increasingly popular. Benefits include:
	•	Better quality hires: Candidates are assessed on what they can actually do, not just their CV/resumé or what they say at interview
	•	More inclusive: Removes barriers for people who may not have had the opportunity to demonstrate their skills in a previous role
	•	Future-ready: Helps understand the talent available as roles evolve, enabling job carving, or even job changes
Skills-based Recruitment
	•	Conduct a skills audit of the role 
	•	Break down each role into the core skills needed to succeed (technical, behavioural, digital, etc.) 
	•	Involve current jobholders and managers to validate the list.  
	•	Focus on essential skills only.

	•	Rewrite Job Descriptions 
	•	Remove degree or "X years' sector experience" requirements (unless legally required) 
	•	Describe the outcomes the role is expected to deliver and the skills needed to achieve them. 
	•	Again, focus on essential skills only.

	•	Introduce Practical Assessments 
	•	Use role-based tasks, simulations, job trials or case studies to test how candidates apply their skills. 
	•	Offer workplace adjustments, so you can assess how candidates would perform in the right conditions.

	•	Support Hiring Managers 
	•	Train managers to focus on evaluating skills rather than credentials. 
	•	Share examples of successful hires made through skills-based approaches to build confidence.

	•	Track and Adjust 
	•	Measure quality of hire, and diversity outcomes over time. 
	•	Gather continual feedback from candidates and managers. 
	•	Review and refine the process regularly.
Inclusive job descriptions
Attract diverse talent by writing job descriptions that are accessible, inclusive, and welcoming to all candidates.
Key principles: 
	•	Clarity 
	•	Relevance 
	•	Flexibility 
	•	Welcoming
Checklist 
	•	Avoid acronyms and jargon 
	•	Use bullet points for readability 
	•	Clearly separate essential and desirable criteria 
	•	State commitment to accessibility and inclusion 
	•	Mention flexibility (e.g. hours, location, ways of working)
Language and tone
Use plain language: 
	•	Avoid complex phrases (e.g., "work with" rather than "liaise with"). 
	•	Avoid corporate buzzwords (e.g., "rockstar," "fast-paced," "give 110%").
Avoid exclusionary language: 
E.g. "must be energetic," "fast learner," or "excellent memory."
Do say: 
"We welcome and support different thinking and working styles"
Inclusive language tips: 
	•	Use identity-first language 
	•	Highlight that there are adjustments available at every stage 
	•	Provide details of all the steps in the recruitment process, including key dates
Role requirements
Avoid unnecessary or overly strict criteria 
Examples: 
	•	Requiring a driver's licence when the job doesn't involve travel or public transport would be possible. 
	•	Listing "excellent verbal communication" as essential for a role that could be done via written or visual communication. 
	•	Asking for specific degrees when equivalent experience would be just as valid. 
	•	Saying "must thrive in a fast-paced environment", which could deter applicants who need a predictable or structured workday.
Example rewrite:
Instead of saying: 
"All applicants must have a valid driver's license"
Or 
"Excellent communication skills are required for the role"
Say: 
"Occasional travel required, accessible transport options available"
and 
"Able to share ideas and collaborate effectively (in writing, verbally, or visually)"
Example structure 
	•	Job title and location 
	•	Summary of role (this can be brief) 
	•	Responsibilities (bulleted list) 
	•	Required skills, strengths and experience (clear, concise) 
	•	Desirable (optional) 
	•	Benefits, flexibility, employee resource groups, examples of adjustments 
	•	Inclusion statement 
	•	Details of the recruitment process 
	•	How to apply, formats of applications, and a named contact for further info or questions
Accessibility top tips! 
	•	Clear headings and bullet points 
	•	Keep paragraphs short (2–4 lines) 
	•	Use sans-serif fonts in at least 12pt

Tips for inclusive interviews
Interviews, used in isolation, may not be the best predictor of future performance (observing candidates carrying out relevant tasks are far more accurate). However, there are many ways to ensure that interviews are as inclusive and accessible as possible.
Before the Interview
Ensure the venue is as accessible as possible, and that all panel members are trained in inclusive interview techniques
	•	Make sure candidates are given adequate notice, and therefore time to prepare. 
	•	Give detailed information about the venue – directions to get there (by public transport and by driving), car parking information, and how accessible the venue is. It can be helpful to include photographs of the entrance to the building, the reception area and the interview room.
	•	Encourage candidates to advise you of any adjustments they might need. 
	•	Describe the format of the interview, and who will be on the panel. 
	•	Send the questions (or at least, the themes) in advance. 
	•	If any additional assessment methods are to be used on the day of the interview, be clear about what these involve so the candidate will know if they need to ask for adjustments for those too.  
	•	Give details of anything you need the candidate to bring with them (for example, if they can bring notes or a companion with them into the interview), and dress code. The latter might sound obvious to most people, but deciding what to wear can be an enormous source of anxiety for some people. 
	•	Make sure that any unavoidable changes are communicated to the candidate in good time, and alternative arrangements described in detail.
During the interview 
Make sure the questions are straightforward, unambiguous, jargon-free and relevant to the role. 
	•	The interviewers should ensure that candidates are evaluated purely on their ability to do the job, not by irrelevant social factors such as amount of eye-contact, or how much they fidget. 
	•	Whilst many interviews are competency-based, explore transferable skills and strengths too. 
	•	It may be helpful for some candidates to be able to bring a support worker or other companion, take time to acclimatise to the room before the interview starts and be given time to process questions and formulate answers.
After the interview 
	•	It is good practice to have a panel of (diverse) interviewers rather than a single person, so the hiring decision is shared. 
	•	Scoring candidates individually against a pre-agreed set of relevant criteria, and then comparing notes after the interviews are completed, means that a robust discussion can be had.
Job offers and inductions
The recruitment process doesn't end with the hiring decision – the job offer has to made (and accepted), unsuccessful candidates need to be informed, and the successful candidate(s) need to be inducted/onboarded.
Job offer
This is pretty straightforward, but just a note to make sure you don't fail at the final hurdle! Make sure the job offer is accessible.
One company did everything brilliantly throughout the recruitment process, ensuring that barriers were removed, and adjustments provided. The best candidate happened to be blind. The company sent him his offer letter, accompanied by a (hard copy) catalogue of assistive technology to browse through. The candidate turned the job down because he had been sent inaccessible information, and they lost their best candidate.
Unsuccessful candidates
A common complaint from candidates, disabled and non-disabled alike, is the lack of feedback from recruiters.
It is unrealistic to be expected to offer personalised, high-quality feedback on every application you receive, but an acknowledgement of receipt and a short, polite rejection for those not short-listed is not unreasonable. For candidates who have been put forward to the next stage, it should be standard practice to offer feedback to those who are unsuccessful, as the numbers will be much lower.
Onboarding/induction
Having recruited your best candidate(s), it's important for them to start their career with you on the right footing. You will need to have an early conversation around any workplace adjustments required in order to reduce or remove the barriers they might face.
The new recruit is likely to know what adjustments will work for them. It might be flexible working hours to avoid the rush hour commute. It might be some specialist software. It might be keeping the walkways clear to avoid a tripping hazard.
If they, or you, are unsure of what adjustments may be helpful, there are plenty of helpful resources out there (including Evenbreak – we can help with workplace adjustment assessments)
Adjustments should be put in place as quickly as possible. An intern's assistive technology didn't arrive until the last day of his internship, meaning he was unable to demonstrate his true skills throughout, and lost out on the opportunity of a permanent job.
Job Carving
Inclusive job carving leads to meaningful work for disabled people and productivity gains for employers.
One of the many beauties of having a diverse workforce is the equally diverse range of skills on offer. Playing to those strengths is when the magic happens.
If someone is great at detailed research and analytics, and hates brainstorming ideas in a team meeting or chatting with clients, let them get on with the research while a colleague great at something else does that. If another person is fantastic at coming up with really creative ideas, don't give them data entry work.
This is relevant whether the person is disabled or not, and is just good talent management. But it also opens up opportunities for disabled people to carry out a role they can excel in, without being expected to do a related task that someone else could carry out.
Top tips
	•	Audit the tasks and skills the role needs to operate successfully
	•	Group tasks into types (e.g., manual, admin, data analysis, report writing etc)
	•	Audit the skills and preferences of the people involved
	•	Discuss and agree tasks with individuals, including any required adjustments
	•	Review regularly to ensure arrangement works for the individuals and the business
Example
A marketing task requires going out and about creating great video content, do detailed editing, and create captions/audio descriptions. It may be that a dyslexic colleague is highly skilled in the creative activities, but not so good at the detail-orientated activities. A solution may be for one person to do the filming and editing, while another person produces captions, thus maximising the skills and time of each person.
Note: 
	•	Don't strip essential functions just to make carving easier 
	•	Ensure equal pay for equal value 
	•	Integrate carved roles fully into team culture

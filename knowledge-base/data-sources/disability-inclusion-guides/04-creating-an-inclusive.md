# Creating an inclusive

Creating an Inclusive Digital Space
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Today, much of our work happens online, through documents, emails, meetings, and shared platforms. If these spaces are not inclusive, some individuals may become excluded.
For colleagues who are disabled or neurodivergent, barriers present in a file, email, or call can make the difference between being enabled to contribute and being shut out.
Remember, creating inclusive digital spaces is:
	•	A legal requirement under equality and accessibility law, organisations must anticipate and remove reasonable barriers.
	•	Good business: inclusion attracts and retains talent, reduces errors, and fosters trust.
	•	Benefits everyone, clear, accessible communication makes work easier and more effective for all.
Inclusive digital practice isn’t an “extra, it’s a fundamental part of how we work together. Therefore, it should be built into the way you work by design.
Creating accessible documents
Headings and structure
	•	Use built-in heading styles.
	•	Don’t just bold or enlarge text. Break up long blocks of writing into clear sections Use bullet points for a list
	•	Ensure accessible spacing of at least 1.5
Fonts and layout
	•	Use a sans-serif font (e.g. Arial, Calibri).
	•	Minimum body of text size 12pt for documents, 18pt for slides, 16pt for email.
	•	Always left-align text, avoid full justification or centred paragraphs - (unless for headings or short call to actions)
	•	Use bold for emphasis, not italics or underlining.
Colour and contrast
	•	Text must stand out against the background.
	•	It is recommended to use an white or pastel background
	•	Don’t rely on colour alone to give meaning.
Did you know?
The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) require a minimum colour contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large or bold text. This ensures that text stands out from the background, making it easier to read. For example, black text on a white background meets the standard, while light grey on white often does not. Good contrast benefits those with low vision, colour blindness, and anyone using small screens or in bright light. Microsoft’s built-in accessibility checker can test these ratios with you.

Tables and images
	•	Only use if necessary - instead opt for paragraphs.
	•	If tables are used do not merge cells and always insert header rows
	•	Add alt text to images.
	•	If decorative, mark as decorative.
PDFs
	•	Make sure PDFs are tagged, searchable, and follow a logical reading order.
	•	Provide navigation (headings, contents, bookmarks). Always check accessibility before sharing.
Accessible presentations
	•	Presentations often create barriers, but they don’t have to.
	•	Use large, clear fonts (minimum 18pt). Keep text short and avoid “wall of words.”
	•	Provide high-contrast visuals, dark text on light backgrounds or vice versa.
	•	Don’t use flashing animations or distracting transitions.
	•	Describe visuals out loud so everyone gets the information.
	•	Share slides in advance in an accessible format.
Digital access isn’t just screen readers
Accessibility is often thought of as “for screen readers. - This isn’t true, people use many different tools:
	•	Voice recognition or dictation software. 
	•	Eye gaze or foot mouse.
	•	Magnifiers and custom display settings. 
	•	Text-to-speech and read-aloud tools. 
	•	Captioning and transcription.
Creating accessible content does not mean being an expert on all products, but designing documents considering those who may not be accessing them traditionally is valuable for everyone.
File sharing and collaboration platforms
Shared platforms like Teams, SharePoint, or Google Drive can either support inclusion or create barriers.
	•	Give files clear, descriptive names.
	•	Avoid uploading scanned images of text; always share text-based files.
	•	Keep folder structures consistent and straight forward.
	•	Provide alternative file formats if needed (e.g. Word as well as PDF).
Email accessibility
Emails are a daily tool, but they can also create common barriers.
	•	Use clear subject lines.
	•	Keep messages short with bullet points or short paragraphs.
	•	Use a sans-serif font at 16pt.
	•	Avoid italics, underlining, or full capitals.
	•	Add alt text to any images or logos.
	•	Always include essential details in the email body, not just attachments.
	•	If the email is long, include an attached version in a plain text document
	•	Offer more than one way to respond.
	•	Signpost additional support and alternative formats.

Meetings and online culture
Before the meeting
	•	Share the agenda and materials in advance.
	•	Ask about access needs (captions, interpreters, alternative formats).
	•	Suggest joining via the desktop app for the best accessibility.
During the meeting
	•	Ensure a distraction-free background. 
	•	Always enable live captions.
	•	Spotlight interpreters so they remain visible. 
	•	Provide instructions on ways to participate. 
	•	Read out messages from the chat.
	•	Describe any key visuals on slides.
	•	Encourage that microphones are muted unless speaking.
After the meeting
	•	Provide notes or transcripts.
	•	Share recordings with captions possible.
	•	Create space for feedback and questions.
Communication channels and digital etiquette
Inclusive communication isn’t just about what we write it’s also about how we use digital channels. Clear, respectful habits help everyone manage information without overwhelm.
Respecting availability
	•	Pay attention to status indicators (e.g., “busy,” “away,” “do not disturb”) and avoid interrupting unless the matter is urgent.
	•	If you need a response, make clear whether it is urgent or can wait. Add [Urgent] or [For later] in the message subject line or first line.
	•	Be patient, not everyone can reply instantly.
Managing channels
	•	Limit the number of chat groups or channels. Too many options can make things unclear and lead to missed messages.
	•	Clearly label what each channel is for (e.g. #Team- Updates, #Quick-Questions, #Social).
	•	Use the right channel for the right purpose. Don’t share urgent project updates in a casual chat space.
Making messages accessible
	•	Keep messages short and structured, with one idea per message, which helps people process and respond effectively.
	•	Avoid jargon, slang or unexplained abbreviations.
	•	If using emojis, GIFs, or images, keep them limited and save them for the end of a message. Add a short explanation so that no one misses the meaning.
	•	Consider agreed-upon tone indicators and create a guide, eg /jk or /sar, for just kidding and sarcastic.
Role modelling accessibility and identity
	•	Microsoft Teams and other platforms allow you to share accessibility needs in your profile or meeting settings, for example, flagging that you require captions. Encourage colleagues to use these features so that their needs are clear without the need for repeated explanations. However this should remain voluntary.
	•	Colleagues can also include pronouns in their display names if they wish. This helps create a respectful, inclusive culture.
	•	Encourage team members to share communication preferences, for example, “I prefer written follow-up after meetings” or “Please email me instead of chat for detailed questions.”
Setting norms as a team
	•	Managers should agree with teams on how channels will be used and model good habits.
	•	Review etiquette regularly, as what worked six months ago may not work now.
	•	Encourage colleagues to speak up if a channel or method isn’t working for them.
Language
	•	Language shapes how people experience your workplace, so consider:
	•	Using Plain English
	•	One idea, one sentence.
	•	Using the Active voice: “We will send the report” instead of “The report will be sent.”
	•	Using Everyday words: “help” not “assist.”
	•	An average Reading age of 12–13, this helps most people, especially those whose English isn’t their first language.
Inclusive wording
	•	Respect pronouns and identities.
	•	Avoid phrases like “suffers from” or “wheelchair bound.”
	•	Always explain acronyms the first time. Use descriptive links instead of “Click here.”
	•	Use identity-first language (e.g., “disabled person”) unless someone shares a personal preference.

QUICK WINS VS CONTINUOUS PRACTICE
Inclusive digital spaces aren’t created once they’re built; they're maintained through habit.
	•	Run the accessibility checker before sharing document.
	•	Add alt text to images.
	•	Use plain English in emails and documents.
	•	Use sans serif fonts.
	•	Regularly review documents and platforms for barriers.
	•	Check in with colleagues about what works for them.
	•	Invest in training, reviews and tools.
	•	Create a feedback process with an evaluation stage.
	•	Build inclusion into policies, not just individual efforts.
Quick checklist
	•	Run the accessibility checker before sharing any document.
	•	Add alt text to all images (mark decorative ones as decorative).
	•	Use sans serif fonts, and use plain English in documents, emails, and messages.
	•	Share your accessibility needs or communication preferences in your profile or with your team.
	•	Respect availability indicators (e.g. “busy,” “away”) and avoid unnecessary interruptions.
	•	Label chat channels clearly and use the right one for the right purpose.
	•	Mark message priority clearly e.g. [Urgent] or [For later].
	•	Keep messages concise, structured, and free from jargon.
	•	Share agendas and materials in advance of meetings.

### Video transcript: Meghan's lived experience

Meghan:

Hi, I'm Meg. My pronouns are she/her. I'm the Head of Training, Consultancy and Innovation at Evenbreak. I'm a white woman in my mid- twenties with medium length brown hair, and I'm wearing black jeans and a black T-shirt. I'm gonna talk a little bit about my own lived experience, which is hugely varied and multifaceted, so I'll talk about different parts of it in this section today.

For me, I'm a disabled and neurodivergent woman. And that means that I live at the intersection of both disabling barriers from a physical access perspective and a chronic pain perspective, but also from a neurodivergency perspective, which means the way in which I think about things is different

For me day to day, some of the barriers that I experience relate to living with pain, needing to move around a lot. You'll see as we record this video that I sway a lot as I'm speaking. The other barriers that I experience relate to how I process information, how I can focus in meetings.

So for me to be able to thrive in the workplace, the adjustments are quite simple. I need to be able to move around, and that includes when I'm working at my desk. I spend a lot of time sitting at a desk, so I have a standing desk that allows me to swap between sitting and standing. I also, for my own neurodivergency, as a dyslexic individual, use a lot of assistive technology day to day.

My favorite piece of assistive technology is my automatic spell checker, which runs and sits between my keyboard and my computer, and automatically processes any typos and spelling mistakes that I've made. When I checked this week, it had made over 30,000 corrections in the last year, and it's such a simple and enabling way to enable me to continue to do my work without much difference or impact.

I also experience cluster headaches, and that means that I intermittently lose part of my hearing and vision, and that fluctuates. It's difficult for me to predict when that might happen, so as adjustments, my team know that I might need to step out if we're working in person, or I might need to sit down for a few moments.

It also means that on my computer, I've got a really wide range of technology. That means that I can use my computer both as a screen reader, but also as a magnifier. I can also put a filter on one of my screens to make it easier for me to process the text.

When my adjustments are put in place, when my barriers are removed, I'm able to work just as any other non-disabled person, but without those adjustments, without those quite simple, small changes, I would really struggle to work day to day.

### Video transcript: Adam's adjustments in the workplace

Adam:

Hi there, I'm Adam Hyland, I work for Evenbreak.

So what do I need in terms of reasonable adjustments in the workplace? Well, to start with, I'm a wheelchair user, so if we're working in places I need to be able to work in environments that are wheelchair accessible. I'm also supported by a personal assistant so that needs to be taken into consideration in terms of space to be able to work side by side at the computer.

And I'm also neurodivergent and therefore I use text to speech to read my emails and reports and documents aloud because I can process information a lot quicker by auditory processing compared to reading. And by me, I use a piece of assistive technology, which enables me to control the computer using my foot, and that is called a trackball.

When I have all these things in place, I know I can thrive.

# Self advocacy and e

Self-Advocacy and Employee Resources
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Self-advocacy is the ability to express one's needs, request support, and make informed choices. For disabled and neurodivergent employees, self-advocacy is often essential to accessing adjustments and thriving at work. Yet many people find it difficult to share their needs due to stigma, fear of discrimination, or past negative experiences. Without a safe and supportive environment, employees may mask challenges, leading to burnout, disengagement, or reduced career progression.
Advocating for your rights
A guide for employees
	•	Prepare ahead of time – Review your organisation's adjustment processes, identify your barriers and strengths, and think about possible adjustments or solutions.
	•	Know your rights – Research the legal responsibilities around workplace adjustments so you're clear on what support you're entitled to.
	•	Be collaborative – Stay open to your employer's ideas as well as your own, and aim for a constructive, problem-solving approach.
	•	Communicate in the way that works best for you – Whether that's face-to-face, by phone, or in writing; you can also bring someone with you for support.
	•	Stay focused on barriers, not diagnosis – Clearly explain the challenges you face and how adjustments will help, and ensure next steps are agreed and written down.
	•	If you face resistance – Remind your employer that adjustments are a legal right, often low-cost or funded, and that only a court can decide if a request is 'reasonable'.

Employee Resources that Make a Difference
	•	Employee resource groups (ERGs) – Peer-led groups for disabled staff to share experiences, provide mutual support, and influence organisational policy.
	•	Access to coaching and mentoring – Specialist workplace strategy coaching, peer mentoring, or reverse mentoring programmes can empower employees and raise awareness among leaders.
	•	Wellbeing and support services – Counselling, occupational health, and Employee Assistance Programmes (EAPs) tailored to disability inclusion.
	•	Resource hubs – Centralised, easy-to-access guidance on adjustments, legal rights, and support options.
	•	Awareness training – Regular sessions that encourage all employees to understand adjustments, communication styles, and inclusive practices.
Access Passports
An Access Passport is a confidential document that records an employee's access needs, adjustments, and preferred ways of working. It provides a simple, consistent way for employees to share what support helps them thrive, without needing to repeatedly explain personal details. For leaders and managers, it's a practical tool to ensure that agreed adjustments are understood, implemented, and carried forward if the employee changes roles or managers.
By focusing on barriers and solutions rather than medical details, an access passport helps create a more inclusive, supportive, and respectful workplace where everyone can contribute at their best.
Building a Culture that Supports Self-Advocacy
A guide for leaders
Organisations play a critical role in making it easier for employees to speak up. Key enablers include:
	•	Psychological safety – When leaders create an environment of trust and respect, employees are more likely to share what they need.
	•	Clear processes – Simple, transparent systems for requesting adjustments or support reduced stress and uncertainty.
	•	Normalising difference – Highlighting that everyone works differently helps position adjustments as routine, not exceptional.
	•	Role models and allies – Senior staff or peers who openly share their experiences can help others feel comfortable advocating for themselves.
Common Barriers to Self-Advocacy
A guide for leaders
	•	Fear of stigma – Worries about being judged or treated differently.
	•	Complex systems – Adjustment processes that require excessive paperwork or medical proof.
	•	Manager inexperience – Leaders who lack training may unintentionally dismiss or mishandle requests.
	•	Isolation – Without networks or visible role models, employees may feel they are the only one facing barriers.
Top Tips for enabling self-advocacy
A guide for leaders
	•	Provide training for managers on how to respond constructively to adjustment requests.
	•	Share real examples (with consent) of successful adjustments to normalise asking for support.
	•	Ensure confidentiality and choice in how employees share their needs.
	•	Celebrate employee networks and give them visibility and influence.
	•	Embed feedback loops so employees can safely raise concerns if processes are not working.
3 Quick wins for leaders
	•	Ask proactively – Include access needs in onboarding, team check-ins, and performance reviews, not just when a problem arises.
	•	Signpost resources – Make sure all staff know where to find ERGs, policies, and adjustment guidance.
	•	Model openness – Share your own examples of times you needed support or flexibility—it shows that asking is normal.

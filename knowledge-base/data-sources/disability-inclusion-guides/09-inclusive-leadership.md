# Inclusive leadership

Inclusive Leadership
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Being an inclusive leader isn’t about having all the answers, it’s about staying curious, creating safety, and being willing to learning and adapting. This guide offers practical steps to help you do just that, by recognising biases, building flexible team cultures, and leading with equity and empathy.
Designed for everyday use, this guide will support you to:
	•	Practice inclusive leadership in everyday actions
	•	Build trust and psychological safety
	•	Support flexible working and access needs
	•	Reflect and grow as a leader
Whether you’re just starting out or already championing inclusion in your role, this guide is designed to move you forward, one action at a time.
Understanding your role as a leader 
How to reflect on your own privileges and biases 
One of the first steps you can take towards being an inclusive leader, is acknowledging the power that you hold to create change. But you must first understand your own privileges and biases and reflect on them intentionally, regularly, and without defensiveness. 
Here are two exercises you can use to deepen your self- awareness, and move from reflection to action: 
Exercise 1: Privilege map
Draw a circle divided into 10 segments for different protected characteristics (e.g. race, religion, gender, background, disability, age, sexuality)
For each characteristic, ask yourself ‘have I ever felt excluded because of this?’, or ‘is this an area where I may hold privilege?’
Colour code each segment, one colour for privilege, another colour for disadvantage.
Around each segment, note how privilege or bias might shape your leadership, communication, or assumptions, and where you need to seek other perspectives to better support your team
Exercise 2: Bias in action
Over two weeks, write short reflections on moments of snap judgments, comfort/discomfort with teammates, or negative reactions.
For each, ask: What was my instinct? Whose needs was I prioritising? Could bias or privilege be at play?
End with a reframe: What could I do differently next time? What question could I ask instead of assuming?
Completing these two exercises regularly helps reveal bias, challenge assumptions, and strengthen fair, inclusive leadership.
Practical strategies for defining equity and inclusion, and what it means for your team.
First, two simple definitions:
Equity = everyone has different needs and faces different barriers, and therefore needs an individualised, tailored approach to support.
Inclusion = addressing existing disadvantages, and taking anticipatory action to ensure that everyone is celebrated, and supported to thrive.
In order to understand what equity and inclusion looks like for your team, it’s important to explore these concepts with them, collaboratively.
Build a shared language that each member of the team co- creates, and make it relevant to the work they do, by asking questions like:
	•	What does inclusion look and feel like here? What values guide us as a team?
	•	What actions will we commit to regularly for continued growth/learning?
	•	How can we support each other’s strengths and needs?
	•	What would make this space feel unsafe for us and how do we prevent it?
	•	How will we hold each other accountable?
How inclusion shapes wellbeing, performance, and innovation
Inclusion drives successful, fulfilled, high-performing teams by ensuring every voice is heard and valued. It means listening to diverse experiences, even when feedback is challenging, and being willing to act on it. Here are two simple ways to understand its impact on your team.
Listen to lived experiences
Create regular safe spaces, including one to one check in’s with each team member, group team calls, and anonymous surveys or forms, and ask team members to share:
	•	Do they feel heard, valued, and able to ask for support when needed?
	•	What gets in the way of their being able to contribute or thrive in their roles?
	•	Are there times they have held back feedback, ideas, or opinions? If so, why?
Audit your team dynamics and successes
Alongside asking for your team’s feedback, observe their behaviours and participation over a period of time:
	•	Who speaks up, gets heard, or receives recognition?
	•	What outside factors may affect performance and require adjustments?
	•	Who is consistently meeting targets and deadlines, and who potentially needs a little more help or support?
Lack of inclusion in the workplace leads to poor wellbeing in both individuals and teams, which often shows up as silence, withdrawal, and burn-out.
By noticing the patterns that appear in both your one to one conversations with team members, and your observation audits, you’ll begin to see reflections of deeper structural or cultural exclusion in your workplace.
You can then use this to begin making positive changes, such as implementing adjustments like flexible hours or reduced workload, different ways of contributing in meetings to ensure everyone feels comfortable to participate, and stronger project delegation and management among team members.

Creating your leadership style
Anticipatory communication best practice for supporting new employees.
It’s important that leaders create an inclusive and accessible experience for employees throughout their whole career - including before they’ve even started in their new role.
Here’s a really practical list of things you can do to achieve an accessible onboarding process, and set your employees up for success:
Before a new employee starts
	•	Ask about access needs early: “How do you work best?” or “What can we do to ease your transition?”
	•	Provide information in multiple formats: written guides, video calls, audio summaries, calendar invites.
	•	Share a clear first-week plan in advance, including schedule, meetings, and systems to learn.
	•	Introduce the team beforehand with names, roles, pronouns, and a brief ‘meet the team’ page.
	•	Check access to accounts and any assistive technology they may need.
During early communication
	•	When your new employee is settling in use clear and concise language, avoiding any jargon where possible.
	•	Ask what their communication preference is - would they prefer emails and written documents? Would they prefer to hop on a call and then receive a written summary after it’s been verbally explained?
	•	Check in regularly and privately - ask: ‘is anything feeling unclear?’ and ‘is there anything you’d like more time or support with?’.
Ongoing support and belonging
	•	Be transparent about the feedback process - set expectations with them about how and when they’d like to receive feedback.
	•	Explicitly commit to ongoing inclusion - let them know that it’s a priority to make sure they belong and thrive, and that they can ask for any support at any time.
	•	Reiterate that change is ok - their access needs may change at any time, and it’s important that they can talk to you if something is no longer working.
Collaborating with your team to find new ways of working
We all know as leaders that our team members are unique individuals who bring their own strengths to a role, and also face their own challenges. It therefore makes sense, they would have their own individual ways of working too.
As a leader, you can support inclusive, flexible working by:
	•	Ask about access needs early and often – don’t wait for someone to struggle; ask what helps them work at their best and what adjustments make tasks more accessible.
	•	Offer choice, not permission – adjustments should be actively encouraged, not given as a favour, giving team members real agency over their work.
	•	Co-create a living ‘ways of working’ guide – include preferred communication styles, working hours, tools, and access needs, updating it as the team evolves.
	•	Balance individual needs with team participation – when needs conflict, work together to find solutions that suit everyone.
	•	Lead by example – share how you work best and what adjustments you use, normalising flexibility and access for the team.
	•	Recognise that needs change over time – regularly check in and adapt to ensure everyone continues to work effectively.
A Life-Long Leadership Practice
Best practice for seeking and implementing feedback for your inclusive leadership style
The best way to know if the accessibility and inclusion initiatives you are trying with your team are actually working, is to seek their feedback. Psychological safety is the foundation for true and honest feedback, and so it’s important to create a culture where people can say what they genuinely think, without fear, repercussion or reprimand.
Here’s some best practice to help you achieve this:
Make it safe to be honest
Frame questions to reassure your team there are no negative consequences. For example: “I value your input and appreciate any honest reflections.” Provide safe spaces to share through 1:1s, anonymous forms, or pulse surveys focused on your leadership.
Act on what you hear
When feedback is shared, listen openly and without defensiveness. Take visible action and let your team know what’s changing.
For example: “Based on your feedback, I’ll be doing X differently. Please let me know how it’s going in a few weeks.”
Keep feedback ongoing
Inclusion is continuous. Let your team know you’re always open to feedback and willing to adjust to find what works best.
Top five ways to stay curious and keep growing
Learn with your team, not for your team
Inclusion is built together. Hold regular meetings to discuss what’s working, organize training for everyone, or let individuals lead learning moments on relevant topics or lived experiences.
Engage with lived experience
Follow disabled advocates and equity-focused thinkers, read blogs, listen to podcasts, and use platforms like SCOPE, Disability Rights UK, or Evenbreak to stay informed.
Build in structured learning time
Set aside regular CPD hours for the team to develop inclusion skills—learning assistive tech, attending inclusive leadership coaching, or exploring strategies to overcome barriers.
Join or create a peer resource space
Connect with inclusive leadership networks, newsletters, mentors, or set up check-ins with other leaders—learning inclusion is best done collaboratively.
Reflect and reframe ‘failure’ as growth
Inclusive leaders reflect on mistakes: ask what was missed and what to do differently, then share learnings openly to build psychological safety.

Closing thoughts
Inclusive leadership is not a one-time achievement, it’s a continuous, evolving practice. It’s about showing up with intention, listening deeply, challenging systems that create disadvantage, and creating space for everyone to thrive.
You don’t need to be perfect. What matters most is that you stay committed to learning, take action where you can, and remain open to feedback, especially when it’s uncomfortable. Inclusive leaders lead with humility, curiosity, and courage.
Remember: inclusion is built in the everyday moments, how you run meetings, how you give feedback, how you respond to someone asking for support. Those small actions create the culture your team experiences.
Let this guide be a starting point, not a finish line. Keep listening, keep growing, and keep leading the way.

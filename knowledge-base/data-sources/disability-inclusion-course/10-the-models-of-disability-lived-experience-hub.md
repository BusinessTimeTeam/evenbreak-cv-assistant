### Video transcript: My journey with the social model

Meghan:

For me, it's taken a really long time to become comfortable with the social model of disability, and that's because, as somebody who was newly diagnosed with a long-term health condition and a chronic pain condition, it took me a long time to understand that my own reference point for disability was just not that inclusive.

Initially I identified as a person with a disability, and I did that because of my own internalised disableism about what it really meant to be disabled in the modern day world. So for me, what that looked like was disassociating my disability, the concept of disability from my own self. But nowadays I identify as a disabled person because I understand the social model.

I understand that my own impairment to use social model language, my own lived experience is innately different from how society views me as a disabled person. And that was really game changing for me.

To go on that journey and realise that actually it's the world around me that disables me, and therefore I'm entitled to share my access needs and to expect that adjustments will be made to accommodate me in any given environment, whether that's a restaurant or in the workplace.

And that, for me, was really enabling, and I hope is a journey that many people who are new to the um, world of disability can also go on too.

### Video Transcript: Overcoming the Main Challenges

Jane:

I think one of the main challenges is that a lot of companies don't even think about the benefits of employing disabled people, so the question doesn't even arise.

When it does arise, I think the two main barriers that people face is just overwhelm. Where do I start? What do I do first? I don't even know where to begin with this, and the second one is the fear of getting it wrong. What happens if we do something and it doesn't work, or we say the wrong thing or we offend people.&nbsp;

And I think the way to overcome those barriers is to really use lived experience. So really use people who have lived experience of disability that can help you with this, so you don't have to do this on your own. You don't have to be the expert.&nbsp;

Doesn't really matter where you start, just start somewhere. And, just make sure that you sense check it with people with lived experience to make sure that the things you're doing really are working in practice.

### Video Transcript: Identifying and Addressing Barriers

Jane:&nbsp;

I think the easy way to identify if there are barriers that exist is through monitoring. So if you already have disabled people at every level within the organisation, if they're thriving, if they're productive, then you're probably doing everything okay.

The likelihood is that that isn't the case, and so monitoring is one way that you can identify where the barriers are. Another way is by talking to disabled employees who already work for you and saying to them, what works for you and what doesn't work for you? And using that lived experience to give you feedback on what's working and what isn't working is a really good place to start in how you improve on what you're doing already.

Now, of course, it may be that you don't have many disabled people already working for you, in which case you have a bit of a problem, but also you've identified there's a problem.

But fear not, there are lots of organisations out there who can help you to take that first stepping point, to look at your processes, to perhaps do a review, see what the barriers might be and make recommendations to reduce them or remove them altogether: of course, Evenbreak is one of those organisations, but there are others.

### Video Transcript: Barriers at Work - Jane's Example

Jane:&nbsp;

So there are many barriers of course that disabled people face in the workplace. That's the whole definition of being disabled. Mine was a bit unusual. I was unable to sit for many years due to a spinal condition, and so I needed to find a way of running a business without being able to sit at a desk.

So we came up with the solution of me lying flat on a platform and then having a laptop suspended above me so that I could actually work lying down. It's not as uncommon now as it was then, but at the time it was fairly unusual.

### Video Transcript: Actions from Leadership

Jane:&nbsp;

The leadership role is crucial in promoting disability inclusion, and there are lots of things that leaders can do, and they don't need to be experts in this field. What they do need to be is open and curious and willing to learn, and so they can model good practice in terms of the way that they behave, the way that they work. They can make sure that they include the lived experience of disability within strategy. So if they have, for example, an employee resource group, going to that employee resource group and asking their opinion on:

"Does this work?"

"How would this work for you?"

"What might the problems be here?"&nbsp;

Also, I think for leaders, it's sometimes quite difficult to be vulnerable. And I think if leaders can be openly vulnerable and say, I don't know what the answers are. I need you to help me or if they can be open about any hidden differences that they may have.

So if they have mental health issues or they have a hidden disability, to be open about that, it feels risky, but actually what it does, it opens up everybody else in the organisation to feel that psychological safety about if they can be open about their condition, it's okay for me to be open about mine.

So it's about fostering that whole culture where everybody within the organisation feels valued, feel supported, is curious, wants to learn and has that continuous improvement where we're always learning how to become more inclusive and more accessible.

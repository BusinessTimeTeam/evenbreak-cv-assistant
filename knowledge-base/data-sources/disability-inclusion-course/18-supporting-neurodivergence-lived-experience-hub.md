### Video transcript: What having ADHD means to me

 Robbie:

So a few weeks ago, following a five month process of phone calls, assessments, and questionnaires for me, my wife and my mum, a psychiatrist, confirmed what I'd expected for a while. I have, and I've always had ADHD, otherwise known as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, now having dived into books, videos, and podcasts on this subject over the last 18 months after self-diagnosing myself on TikTok of all places, it didn't come as much of a surprise.

Finally, being able to put a name to the way that I think and feel has been liberating. So many things that have happened throughout my life suddenly make sense. People with a ADHD don't produce enough of three key chemicals: serotonin, dopamine, and adrenaline. Now that deficiency can lead to a lack of focus, verbal outbursts, and destructive decision making.

This can often mean poor time management problems prioritising, restlessness, problems, completing tasks, and trouble coping with stress, all things I've experienced. But what I now know is that exercise, good sleep and healthy eating habits can boost the production of those chemicals and help ADHD brains operate at their maximum output.

Now, when that happens, creativity and productivity usually follow. Now I've always been wired a little bit differently, herald as the creative one with lots of great ideas. Problem is, I usually get too distracted with the next idea before I fully finish the last one. As a child, I was nicknamed Gobby Robbie by my English teacher for talking too much, had a terrible habit of interrupting people and struggled with big, long projects. But ADHD had never crossed my mind back then. I still remember teachers who said that ADHD didn't exist back when they were younger, and it was a smack and some discipline that was needed. Back when I was at school, it was seen as a glorified label for thick and naughty kids.

Now I was no angel as a child. In fact, my parents have a full back catalog of naughty Robbie stories that they love to tell my daughters, nieces and nephew. But overall I did well at school. I was in the top sets. I got 13 GCSEs, four A-levels and a degree. I mastered the art of revising the night before an exam and ace in it the next day.

Turns out the dumb or stupid stigma is a complete myth. Having ADHD has nothing to do with a person's intellectual capabilities. Some might struggle academically, but this is usually due to challenges with focus or organisational related symptoms, not their ability. In fact, many people with ADHD excel in creativity, problem solving, and out of the box thinking. As Faster Than Normal author Peter Shankman says, this is the last time that I'll use the word diagnosed with regards to my ADHD because I'm not seeing it as a negative.

People with ADHD aren't broken, defective, or damaged. There are hundreds of thousands of successful people thriving in their lives thanks to, not despite, their ADHD. Our brains work differently. We have to adjust to that, but when we do, it can be amazing. I look at my ADHD as a blessing, not a curse.

It's a big part of who I am and helps me to be the best and most creative version of me, as long as I can manage it properly.

### Luci Drury - my journey to understanding: late diagnosis of ADHD

For most of my life, it felt like I was swimming against the tide. Tasks that seemed effortless for others left me feeling overwhelmed and drained. I struggled with daily routines, staying organised, and meeting deadlines — no matter how hard I tried. It wasn’t until recently that I finally discovered the reason behind these lifelong challenges: I was diagnosed with ADHD at the age of 44.

Growing up, I was often labelled “day dreamer" or "lazy." Despite putting in significant effort, I found it incredibly difficult to stay on top of things. These difficulties persisted into adulthood, impacting both my career and personal life. For years, I was diagnosed with anxiety — a label that never quite fit, and deep down, I knew something else was going on.

As a woman, my symptoms were often overlooked or misunderstood. ADHD is still widely associated with hyperactive young boys, and the quieter, inattentive presentation — common among women — often goes undetected. I wasn’t an exception. Over the years, I developed various coping mechanisms: working excessively hard, relying on last-minute adrenaline, and masking my struggles just to keep up.

At school, the clear structure and consequences helped me succeed. I was seen as a high-achieving student because the expectations were well-defined, and I met them. But when I transitioned to college — a far less structured environment — my performance declined. Without the same level of accountability, and without anyone recognising my potential, I struggled.

Before my diagnosis, I often worried about work-life balance and whether something would trigger anxiety. Filling out equality and diversity forms could be particularly difficult. I’m proud to say I have ADHD, and I would gladly tick a box that identifies me as neurodivergent. But when the only option is to declare a "disability," I would feel conflicted. I don’t see myself as disabled, yet I also don’t want to be dishonest.

Looking back, I can now see that I thrived in roles that offered structure, support, and multiple ways to learn — and struggled in those that lacked them. Certain practices are particularly helpful: having a safe, supportive workplace where people feel able to ask for help, and a culture that genuinely values wellbeing and inclusion. I’ve been fortunate to have line managers who took the time to understand me and gave me the space to talk through concerns. Importantly now, they never see my ADHD as a deficit.

In practical terms, I thrive with flexibility. Working from home allows me to manage distractions and move around when needed. Being able to structure my diary so I can balance socially demanding days with quieter admin ones has been a game-changer. Flexible working and the ability to easily book leave have also played a big role in helping me manage my wellbeing.

I wouldn’t change having ADHD — it’s part of who I am, and it comes with real strengths. I’m resilient. I bring fresh perspectives. I have a deep sense of empathy and build rapport easily. I’m calm in a crisis. What I wish, though, is that I hadn’t spent so many years feeling like I was failing at things others found easy. That contradiction — being able to tackle complex tasks, yet struggling with the simplest ones — was deeply frustrating.

Today, I’m fortunate to be in work that truly aligns with my values and strengths. I get to be myself, and I’m doing things that matter.

Receiving a late diagnosis of ADHD has been life-changing. It’s helped me be kinder to myself, understand how my brain works, and find strategies that actually work for me. I’ve learned to prioritise self-care, set achievable goals, and connect with others who understand this journey.&nbsp;

Most importantly, I’ve learned that my story isn’t unusual. Many women go undiagnosed until adulthood, and it's crucial we continue to raise awareness of the diverse ways ADHD can present. If sharing my experience helps even one person feel seen, or prompts someone to seek support, then telling my story has been worthwhile.

Now, I’m proud to support others as a coach and trainer — helping to challenge the stigma and misconceptions around neurodiversity. My journey may have taken time, but it’s brought me to a place of self-understanding, acceptance, and purpose.

### Autism: What helps me do my best work

I am autistic, and for a long time I didn’t realise how much that shaped my experience at work. I knew I found some things harder than other people seemed to, for example, busy offices, unclear expectations, seemingly constant interruptions, but I also knew I brought strengths like deep focus, honesty, deep thinking and attention to detail. It took years before I had the language to understand that both were connected.

In the workplace, my autism affects how I process information, communicate and manage sensory input. Open-plan offices can be overwhelming because of noise, movement and visual distractions. Meetings with vague agendas or unspoken expectations are stressful, because I rely on clarity and structure. Social dynamics like small talk often take far more energy for me than the actual job itself.

The adjustments that help me most are often simple but make a huge difference. Having clear written instructions instead of verbal-only requests reduces anxiety and misunderstandings. Being allowed to use noise-cancelling headphones or work in a quieter space helps me focus. Flexibility around how and when I work, including remote or hybrid working, allows me to manage my energy and sensory load. Knowing what’s expected of me, how my performance is measured, and being given feedback directly rather than indirectly makes work feel safer and more manageable.

As a woman, my experience of autism has been shaped by gender expectations. I spent years masking and smiling through discomfort, because autistic traits in women are often overlooked or misunderstood. At work, this meant I was seen as “coping” while I was actually quietly burning out. When I did have noticeable challenges, it was more likely to be interpreted as anxiety, sensitivity or a confidence issue rather than a need for adjustments.

Gender also affects how autistic behaviour is perceived. When I am direct, it can be perceived as abrupt. When I need clarity, it can be seen as insecurity or as though I don't know what I'm doing. When I withdraw to manage overload, it can be interpreted as disengagement. These assumptions make it harder to ask for support and easier to be misunderstood.

What has helped most is working in environments where neurodiversity is understood as a difference, not a deficit. Managers who ask “What helps you do your best work?” instead of making assumptions have made a lasting impact. Being believed when I explain my needs, and not having to repeatedly justify them, has allowed me to contribute fully without constantly managing myself to fit in.

Autism doesn’t just affect how I work, it affects how I experience work. With the right adjustments and understanding, I can thrive. Without them, even a supportive role can become exhausting. Inclusion, for me, isn’t about lowering expectations, it’s about removing unnecessary barriers so I can meet them in my own way.

### Jesse King: Riding the ADHD wave

For me, ADHD feels like having 12 brains all firing at once, each with its own narrative, ideas, and thoughts competing for attention. It can be intense and overwhelming; it never really slows down. My mind is always buzzing, which makes it both a place of brilliant creativity and exhausting chaos.

I often find myself diving headfirst into a new idea, whether it’s a creative project at work or a personal interest, with full energy and excitement. I’ll hyperfocus for days or weeks and then, almost without warning, I lose all interest. It’s as if the thing I was once obsessed with never existed. I can even feel a sense of aversion to it. That cycle makes it difficult to follow things through to the end, even when I really want to.

What helps me is working on shorter projects with clear timelines. Smaller tasks are easier to engage with, and I’m more likely to complete them while I’m still energised by them. Longer-term projects, especially those with lots of moving parts or multiple milestones, can feel overwhelming. It’s hard to know what to tackle first, and I can lose sight of the big picture.

One thing that really helps me manage longer projects is using Kanban-style tools. I treat each task like it lives on its own little island, so I can stay focused on one piece at a time without being swallowed by the bigger picture. I’ve found that really helpful for calming the mental noise.

I also benefit from regular check-ins with my manager. It gives me a chance to talk through any worries, especially if I’ve been spiralling into overthinking or catastrophising. A task that feels enormous in my head is often much more manageable once I say it out loud.

For me, ADHD and anxiety go hand in hand. Managing one often supports the other. That’s why regular breaks, grounding techniques, and even meditation (basically the opposite of hyperfocus!) have become crucial for helping me stay centered and in control.

### Dyspraxia at work

People often think dyspraxia just means being clumsy. &nbsp;For me, that’s a very small part of it and not the part that affects my working life the most. Dyspraxia impacts how my brain plans, sequences and executes tasks. It affects organisation, memory, communication and processing speed, even though none of that is obvious from the outside.

At work, dyspraxia shows up in ways that don’t fit the stereotypes. I can understand complex ideas quickly but struggle to write them down in the moment. Writing emails, reports or meeting notes takes longer, especially under time pressure. I find it hard to hold multiple instructions in my head at once, and switching between tasks can derail my focus completely. When systems are unclear or processes change without explanation or notice, it takes me longer to adapt because I need time to re-map the steps.

I’ve often felt uncomfortable admitting that these things are difficult. In the workplace, there’s an expectation that you should be organised, confident and able to “figure it out”. Because dyspraxia isn’t widely understood, I’ve worried that asking for support would be seen as making excuses rather than removing barriers. For a long time, I masked the difficulty by overworking and avoiding situations where I might be exposed, like live note-taking, &nbsp;presentations or group work.

What’s made the biggest difference is being able to work differently. Simple adjustments have changed everything. Being given clear priorities and written instructions helps me plan properly. Using assistive technology like speech-to-text and task-management tools reduces the effort it takes to communicate and stay organised. Having agendas in advance and written summaries after meetings allows me to contribute without being overwhelmed by information overload. Flexibility with deadlines, especially for multi-step tasks helps. I also have help with breaking down big projects into clear tasks and sequences I can follow.

The most helpful support I’ve received has come from managers who asked what would make my job easier and also asked how dyspraxia impacts me, trusted me to trial different adjustments and focused on outcomes rather than how I got there.

The Causes of the First World War

The First World War, which began in 1914, had many causes. Historians often group them under four headings: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism.

Militarism describes the build-up of armies and navies in the years before the war. Britain and Germany competed to build ever larger battleships, and the great powers drew up detailed plans for rapid mobilisation.

The alliance system divided Europe into two armed camps. The Triple Entente linked Britain, France and Russia, while the Triple Alliance linked Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy. A dispute between any two powers risked drawing in all the others.

Imperial rivalry over colonies in Africa and Asia added to the tension, and nationalism in the Balkans made the region unstable. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in June 1914 was the spark that set these long-term causes alight.

In conclusion, no single cause explains the outbreak of war. It was the combination of long-term tensions and a short-term crisis that led Europe into conflict.

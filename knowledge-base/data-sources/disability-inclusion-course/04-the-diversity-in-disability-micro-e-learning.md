### Video transcript: Understanding disability

Meghan:

Understanding disability inclusion. Disability is not just about individual experiences or challenges. It's a rich and varied identity that shapes but does not define people's lives. For many, it's also connected to community, creativity and collective pride. It's one of the only marginalised groups that anyone can join at any point in their life.

According to the World Health Organization, over 16% of the global population, more than 1.3 billion people identify as disabled. So disability isn't really a minority. In fact, it's a rich wealth of experiences, insights, skills, and unique perspectives that people can bring to the workplace.

But for us to welcome disabled people into our organisations, we must first understand a little bit about the variety of disabling experiences that people may face in the workplace, and how to ensure that each one is supported and welcomed with genuine belonging.

In this section, we'll explore disability inclusion and identity in its many forms, highlight some common stereotypes and share best practices such as proactive accessibility, flexible design, and recognising disability as a part of human diversity, that ensure that disabled colleagues are fully included and are able to thrive.

### Disability is not homogenous

Within the above categories of disability, there is a huge variety of lived experiences and diagnostic labels. But if we look even deeper than that, each person will experience their own disability and barriers differently. For example, one person's experience of autism may present as finding social situations difficult to navigate, whereas another person's experience of autism may present as differences in the way they think and process information.

Disability is not homogenous

Within the above categories of disability, there is a huge variety of lived experiences and diagnostic labels. Just because two people have the same diagnosis, or 'type' of disability, doesn't mean they will have the same experiences, or need the same supports. In fact, people are complex, and have many facets that make up their lived experiences:

Family background
Socio-economic advantage or disadvantage can affect diagnosis and experience

Race and Religion
Different races and religions can be marginalised and discriminated against

Age and gender
Differences in age and gender identity can be stigmatised and excluded

Sexuality and partnership
Traditional expectations of sexuality and marital status can also cause disadvantage

Intersectionality
Experiences of disability overlap with other identities, often creating additional barriers from multiple disadvantages combined

What's the difference?

Equality
Treating everyone equally means giving everyone the same support regardless of individual circumstance.

Equity

### Your colleague Sarah is a wheelchair user. The team is moving a meeting to another building with steps at the entrance. A manager says, “We’ll just carry Sarah and her chair up the steps so she doesn’t miss out.”

B is correct because inclusion means proactively ensuring equal access. Carrying someone risks safety and dignity, and defaulting to virtual participation sidelines the person instead of addressing the barrier.

- Answer option: A. Carry Sarah and her chair—it’s quicker and shows team spirit.

- **Correct answer:** B. Move the meeting to an accessible space so Sarah can attend equally.

- Answer option: C. Ask Sarah to join virtually instead so the others don’t have to move.

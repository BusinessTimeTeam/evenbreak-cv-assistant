# Deaf inclusion

Deaf Inclusion
A best practice guide from Evenbreak
Introduction
Deafness is not a single, uniform experience - each person’s identity and relationship to hearing differences is unique. Factors that can shape Deaf identity include: 
	•	Age of onset: Being born Deaf (congenital deafness) versus becoming Deaf or hard of hearing later in life (late-deafened) influences language development and cultural identity. 
	•	Technology use: Some individuals may use hearing aids or cochlear implants, while others may not, depending on preference, access, or effectiveness. Not everyone can use hearing aids or cochlear implants, and some people may choose to use their aids in certain situations but not in others. 
	•	Language and culture: Exposure to Deaf culture and sign languages (such as BSL, ASL, or NZSL) can be an important part of identity. Some deaf people, especially who have been deaf since childhood, may have grown up as part of a deaf community who signs, and therefore see themselves as culturally deaf, whereas others may primarily use spoken or written language. 
	•	Personal choice: Not every Deaf or hard of hearing person identifies with Deaf culture; some may connect more with mainstream or hearing communities. Whether or not someone considers themself deaf, chooses to sign or not, uses hearing aids or not, are all personal preferences, which should be respected and never questioned. 
Recognising these differences is essential for avoiding assumptions and ensuring inclusion.
Language 
Deafness exists on a spectrum. This means that what a person can and cannot hear, how they communicate, and the support they use can vary — _between deaf people, but also the situation, environment, or stage of life. The deaf world is large and vibrant! 
Here is a breakdown of some of the main terms used: 
	•	Deaf: those who do not see their deafness as a negative but a strength, and are active with the Deaf community, often using British Sign Language (BSL). 
	•	Deafened: someone who lost their hearing suddenly or later in life, typically as an adult. There is some engagement in a deaf identity, and they may use BSL. The cause could be unknown, due to noise exposure, medication, or a medical condition. 
	•	Hard of hearing: someone who has acquired deafness and still has some residual hearing, and typically without a cultural deaf identity. 
	•	Partially or single-sided deaf: someone who is deaf on one side. 
	•	Deafblind: those with some level of deafness and visual impairment. 

Communication methods and supports 
One of the biggest barriers for deaf people in the workplace is inaccessible communication methods that lead to deaf individuals missing out on important updates and information. 
Whether this is because casual chats happen in the office that exclude deaf people and lead to feelings of isolation, or because meetings, video content and events don’t have accurate captions and transcripts available, or because interpreters haven’t been booked, there is often extra strain and fatigue placed on deaf individuals to try to ‘keep up’ with traditional communication styles that populate most workplaces. 
In terms of more inclusive communication, Deaf and hard of hearing colleagues may use a variety of tools, strategies, and supports to aid with accessibility and understanding. Common methods include: 
	•	Sign language (e.g., BSL, ASL, NZSL) – Sign languages are whole, complete visual languages, with their own grammar, syntaxes and structures. 
	•	Text relay or video relay services – tools that allow real-time interpretation between spoken and signed communication. 
	•	Captions and transcripts – live captions during meetings and accurate transcripts for recorded materials are vital for accessibility. 
	•	Assistive technology – such as hearing loops, speech-to-text software, or amplified telephones. 
	•	Hearing dogs – trained assistance dogs can alert their person to sounds such as alarms, doorbells, or notifications. 
	•	Interpreters - human assistance who specialise in translating between spoken language and sign language, for both parties in any given conversation. 
Offering multiple communication options benefits everyone and helps reduce barriers.
Key considerations for working with interpreters 
Interpreters play a key role in creating accessible communication. It’s vitally important to understand and respect the interpreters role as a professional service to help deaf and hearing people communicate with each other. Often, there is concern about interpreters having security clearance and access to internal information, which limits and prevents access for the deaf person as well, and creates exclusion. 
Interpreters adhere to a strict professional code of conduct, including confidentiality of all information shared, which means that there is no threat to any private information or intellectual property for the company. The interpreter is not there to engage in activities, or share information about their experiences, they are there to support open communication. 
When working with interpreters: 
	•	Speak directly to the Deaf colleague, not the interpreter. This can make the deaf colleague feel invisible, invalidated, and undermined. 
	•	Allow extra time for communication - interpreted conversations can take longer because the interpreter has to translate from the spoken language into the sign language and back again, and some signs are different in different contexts and situations, so the interpreter may have to wait until you have finished speaking before they can interpret the correct translation. 
	•	Share any materials (like agendas or slides) with interpreters in advance to support accurate translation. It can also help to give the interpreter and the deaf person time in advance of meetings or events to introduce themselves and discuss any context or important considerations up front. 
	•	Position interpreters where both they and the Deaf colleague have a clear line of sight. 
Other barriers for deaf colleagues 
Alongside communication barriers, there are many other barriers that can impact deaf individuals and their ability to show up and do their roles with confidence and precision. These barriers include: 
	•	Inaccessible practices and processes - these often start from the recruitment stage and last all throughout the working career, and include things like job descriptions full of jargon, interviews that only test verbal skills and promotion systems that account for different access needs. 
	•	Barriers to reading - much workplace communication, especially for Deaf colleagues, comes in written form (when interpreters aren’t present). Deaf colleagues may need longer to process written information because their first language is visual (sign language), and written forms like English are often a second language. 
	•	Unsuitable environments - whether due to noise levels impacting the ability to use hearing aids, poor lighting affecting the ability to lip read, difficult line of sight to interpreters, or workstations in the middle of the room which may cause startle when people move behind them, deaf colleagues may encounter many barriers when the physical space isn’t built with them in mind. 
	•	Isolation - when there is a lack of understanding of deaf culture, a lack of other signers, and a lack of celebration around pride in deaf identity, it can quickly become a lonely environment for deaf colleagues, and can lead to mental health challenges. 
Work culture and etiquette 
Hearing dogs in the workplace 
As well as communication support, some deaf colleagues may have a hearing dog. 
This is a highly trained assistance dog that supports deaf people by: 
	•	Alerting to important everyday sounds, like phones and doorbells 
	•	Recognising critical danger signals, such as fire alarms 
	•	Providing emotional reassurance and companionship 
	•	Helping with confidence and independence in busy or unfamiliar environments. 
Key things to know: 
	•	Don’t pet, feed or distract them - even if they look relaxed. 
	•	Hearing dogs are working animals, not pets. 
	•	When wearing their distinctive burgundy jackets, they are on duty. 
	•	Policies should make exemptions for assistance dogs, including hearing dogs. 
“When workplaces aren’t deaf aware and say stuff like ’I hope you recover soon’ it makes me feel sad, lonely and affects my mental health.” a deaf employee. 
Workplace inclusion 
	•	Ensure any required communication support is in place and booked ahead of time — this might be a sign language interpreter, lipspeaker, or speech-to-text reporter. 
	•	Include deaf colleagues in all social and work conversations – _avoid leaving them out, even if unintentional, such as by  saying that a comment doesn’t matter. 
	•	Avoid expecting colleagues to make or receive phone calls. 
	•	Adjust your communication style to meet the deaf person halfway. 
	•	Avoid last-minute spoken-only announcements that might be missed. 
	•	Workplace inclusion continued 
	•	Learn and use basic sign language — even just fingerspelling your name shows effort. 
	•	Use written communication to ensure no one misses updates or invites. 
	•	Be mindful of noisy and poorly lit venues, like busy pubs, which are inaccessible. 
	•	Make sure emergency alarms are visual and/or vibrating, as well as auditory. 
	•	Provide captions for all video and audio content, as well as video calls. 
	•	Ask how best to follow up (email summary, notes, etc.) 
	•	Encourage your team to attend deaf awareness training. 

Meetings and digital access 
	•	Share agendas and materials in advance, and with interpreters for preparation. 
	•	Book accessible rooms with good lighting and acoustics. 
	•	Arrange seating in a circle or semi-circle so everyone is visible. 
	•	Ensure only one person speaks at a time. 
	•	Use visual aids, slides, or diagrams to support spoken information. 
	•	Provide written summaries to recap and share important information. 
	•	For remote meetings, use video where possible as face visibility is important. 

Top tips 
For overcoming these barriers with your deaf colleagues 
	•	Make sure information is clear and straightforward: remove company jargon, give a variety of ways to engage, and have captions and transcripts for all audio materials.

	•	Create extra time for reading and processing, and provide inclusive coaching and workplace strategy skills training to help deaf colleagues develop tools and methods that support their differences and needs. 
	•	Ensure sensory friendly environments (quiet spaces, rooms with adjustable lighting, the ability to relocate to a desk in the corner etc) or work from home options are available so that deaf colleagues can tailor environments to suit their preferences. 
	•	Encourage the learning of basic sign-language phrases for all team members, and create networks for deaf colleagues to connect and share their experiences. 

3 quick wins for leaders 
	•	Create meeting etiquette - Provide context and any relevant materials in advance, ensure that team members speak one at a time in meetings and events (to help with lip reading and interpretation), and utilise elements like chat functions to contribute in written formats. Make sure a written follow up is sent of any key actions that were discussed verbally in the meeting. 
	•	Use visual task boards - systems like Trello, Microsoft suite and other visual project management tools can help your team stay connected, receive updates on shared tasks, and collaborate more effectively without there always needing to be a verbal meeting to share progress. 
	•	Create accessible social events - all teams need time to connect with each other outside of their work responsibilities, so when social events are planned, ask yourself: have I asked access needs up front? Have I considered space for interpreters, hearing assistance dogs and other personal supports? 
Final thoughts 
“The best colleagues send action notes after a meeting, and check with me about communication support – interpreters don’t live in my pocket! “– a deaf employee. 
It’s about creating a culture of feeling safe, heard and valued. Every step you take helps build a world where everyone can thrive. 
Don’t panic if you get it wrong: It’s Okay to make mistakes. Just apologise, adjust, and move one. You don’t need to be perfect – just willing to learn and make an effort. 
Many people new to communicating with a deaf colleague can feel nervous, unsure what to do, and worried about saying or doing the wrong thing. The key is to ask, listen, and respect their communication preferences, rather than making assumptions.
Checklist 
	•	I face people when I speak. 
	•	I ask colleagues what works best for them. 
	•	I speak clearly, without shouting or overenunciating. 
	•	I avoid jargon and explain the topic clearly when starting conversations. 
	•	I speak directly to deaf colleagues, even when an interpreter is present. 
	•	I use captions and accessible tech in meetings and video calls. 
	•	I share agendas and follow up with written notes after meetings. 
	•	I check if communication support is needed in advance. 
	•	I make sure video and audio content is always captioned. 
	•	I include deaf colleagues in social chats and work events. 
	•	I consider noise and lighting when planning meetings or informal socials. 
	•	I respect assistance dogs, understand they have legal rights, and not pets. 
	•	I promote inclusive behaviours in my team. 
	•	I encourage deaf inclusion by attending training and sharing resources. 
	•	I have watched lipreading videos to appreciate the challenges deaf people face. 
	•	I have started to learn basic sign language fingerspelling and can sign my name.

Supporting blind/partially sighted colleagues

Introduction
Creating an inclusive workplace means ensuring colleagues who are blind or partially sighted can participate fully and confidently. Inclusion is built not just through policies, but through everyday actions that make communication clearer, meetings more accessible, environments easier to navigate, and support more responsive.
This guide shares practical advice on communication, meetings, workplace environments, sighted guiding, and adjustments that can help remove barriers and promote equity. By adopting these approaches, organisations can foster workplaces where everyone can contribute their skills and thrive.
Communication
Creating accessible communication ensures that blind and partially sighted employees can fully participate, contribute their skills, and access information on an equal basis with others. It is not only about removing barriers but also about fostering a culture of inclusion and respect.
On the following page are practical strategies and accessible formats that can help support greater inclusion in the workplace.
Audio description: Provides spoken commentary of visual details (body language, expressions, actions) during videos or presentations.
Audio formats: Convert key documents (e.g. policies, job descriptions) into audio so people can choose to listen at their own pace.
Braille: Written materials can be produced in braille for colleagues who read by touch.
Large print: Offer documents in at least 18-point font (or larger if requested) using clear fonts and high contrast.
Websites and digital content: Ensure platforms are screen-reader compatible, allow zooming, and support features like dark mode or high-contrast settings.
Meetings
Meetings bring people together to share updates, training, or decisions, but how do you make sure colleagues who are blind or partially sighted are fully included?
Introductions
Start meetings with introductions, especially if people don't know each other. For vision impaired colleagues, add a short visual description, for example:
"Hi, I'm Lee, an Account Manager with Evenbreak. I'm a white male in my mid 30s with black hair."
If you haven't spoken for a while, say your name again as it helps track who's speaking.
Descriptive Language
Don't rely solely on visuals. Describe key content on slides or charts, for example:
"This graph shows profits rising from X to Y." "Here's a photo of the team in the quad."
Clear, simple descriptions help everyone stay on track.
Describe Your Environment
If you are meeting in person give a quick room overview:
"We're seated in a circle around a circular table, refreshments are on the table to your left."
Mention exits, toilets and letting a colleague know that refreshments are on the table nearby means they won't miss out when everyone else starts tucking in to pastries.
Camera Positioning
If you are having a team meeting virtually, let a colleague know if their camera is off, angled, or blurry.
Don't stay silent to be polite, it can cause more embarrassment if they find out later that you were talking to their forehead the entire meeting.
If unsure, ask: "Want a quick camera check?"
Lighting
For a colleague with a vision impairment, bright overhead lights or uncovered windows can make it harder to focus and could even be painful if they have heightened sensitivity.
Likewise, if it's a bit gloomy or there are shadows over people's faces it can be much harder to pick out details.
Soft, even lighting helps everyone.
Final Tip: Ask, Don't Assume
Every vision impairment is different. Check in about what's helpful and adapt as needed.
Environment
Designing an accessible environment helps blind and partially sighted colleagues navigate the workplace with confidence and independence.
The following considerations outline ways to create spaces that are safe, inclusive, and easy to move through.
Lighting and signage: Provide bright, even lighting. Use large, bold, and clear signage. Where possible, add braille.
Labelling: Clearly label objects, equipment, and rooms. Labels can also be provided in braille.
Contrast and colour: Use bright, contrasting colours to highlight hazards (e.g. stair edges, glass doors). This improves safety and navigation.
Consistency: Keep layouts predictable and avoid cluttered spaces, reducing barriers for navigation.
Sighted guiding
Use this quick guide to feel confident when offering help in everyday workplace situations, from guiding someone to a meeting room, through a doorway, or across a busy office space.
The golden rule:
Always ask first what support the person prefers, and explain what you're doing as you guide.
General principles
Ask: Introduce yourself, ask if help is wanted, and offer your arm. Never pull or push.
Position: The person usually holds your arm just above the elbow and walks half a pace behind you.
Communication: Say what's coming up (stairs, doorways, kerbs, narrow spaces) before you reach it.
Guide dogs: Never distract or touch a guide dog. Approach on the side opposite the dog.
Key guiding situations
Setting off
	•	Stand alongside, offer your arm, and walk at a steady pace.
	•	Let them set the walking speed.
Narrow or busy spaces
	•	Say "we'll go single file."
	•	Move your guiding arm behind you so they can follow directly.
Steps, stairs, and slopes
	•	Say "steps up" or "steps down" and pause before the first step.
	•	Walk one step ahead. Tell them when you've reached the final step.
	•	Allow extra time on downward stairs.
Kerbs and roads
	•	Say "kerb up" or "kerb down." Pause at the edge.
	•	Cross straight across, never diagonally. Use crossings where possible.
Doorways
	•	Say if the door opens towards or away.
	•	Try to keep them on the hinge side.
	•	Open with your guiding arm so they can follow the movement.
	•	Avoid revolving doors; use alternatives.
Seating
	•	Describe the chair (arms, height, wheels).
	•	Place your hand on the back or armrest so they can follow and sit safely.
Vehicles (cars, taxis, buses, trains)
	•	Tell them whether it's the front or back seat.
	•	Place your arm on the door handle or frame for them to follow.
	•	On public transport, explain gaps and steps clearly; guide single file.
Other considerations
Declining help: Respect when someone says no, they may just need orientation.
Children: Same as adults, always ask first.
Top tips to remember
	•	Always ask first, never assume.
	•	Explain what's ahead. Even small steps or slopes matter.
	•	Keep a natural rhythm. Don't rush or pull.

Workplace adjustments
Assistive Technology and Tools
	•	Screen readers (e.g. JAWS, NVDA, VoiceOver)
	•	Screen magnifiers or zoom tools
	•	Braille displays or embossers
	•	Voice assistants and dictation software
	•	Accessible document formats (e.g. tagged PDFs, plain text Word docs)
	•	Keyboard shortcuts and accessible navigation tools
	•	High contrast themes or large print settings
Physical Environment Adjustments
	•	Clear, unobstructed walkways
	•	Tactile markers on stairs, doors or key equipment
	•	Consistent desk location (avoid hot desking where possible)
	•	Adjustable desk lighting or dimmable overhead lights
	•	Minimised glare from windows or screens
	•	Well-defined signage (large print, tactile, Braille)
Communication and Information
	•	Verbal descriptions of visual content during meetings or training
	•	Alternative formats: large print, audio, Braille or digital accessible copies
	•	Accessible intranet, systems and learning platforms
	•	Advance access to slide decks or documents so assistive tech can be used/tested
Workplace Practices and Support
	•	Flexible breaks to reduce eye strain or screen fatigue
	•	Orientation for navigating unfamiliar spaces
	•	Buddy system or sighted guide for new environments
	•	Disability Inclusion training for teams
	•	Adjusted emergency evacuation plans
Inclusive Culture and Actions
	•	Don't assume, ask what's helpful
	•	Give people time to find tools or navigate systems
	•	Avoid using too many emoji's if someone is using a screen reader
	•	Never distract a guide dog without permission
	•	Be mindful of your own body language, non-verbal cues like nodding might be missed

### Video Transcript: Mahomed's perspective

Mahomed:

Hi, my name is Mahomed and I am blind. I have no light perception in either eye, and I rely on predominantly electronic communications for my accessibility needs, such as assistive technology on the mobile phone and laptop. I'm not very proficient with braille, and I also have a guide dog to help me get around.

The barriers I face when looking for work include simple things like how do I get to a new office in an unfamiliar environment? What will people think about me when I walk in and need to ask them to show me where the toilet is, or to pour me a cup of coffee? But those things aren't indicative of how well I can do the job. Those are just things I practically can't do because I can't see a new space.

For me, adjustments are so simple. I just need somebody to meet me at the nearest tube station or train station or bus stop so that they can show me the route to the office. I need someone to show me around a new building. I need the same seat because hot desking would mean I'd have to learn new routes all the time.

I need a space for my guide dog and a space for him to go to spend outside. None of those things impact on my ability to do work. They're just practical solutions to help me overcome my sight loss.

### Video Transcript: Lee's perspective

Lee:

Hi, I'm Lee. I live and work with sight loss, which was caused by complications from diabetes. I use terms such as vision impaired, visually impaired, or just blind to describe myself. I lost my sight when I was 29, after many years experience already in the world of work, and some of the biggest barriers I faced were around relearning and reengaging with a world that I was already familiar with, but was now looking on from a much different perspective.

Some of the barriers that a blind or partially sighted person might face during the application process include the accessibility of a website or an application. The website could be really well designed. It could be really bright and colourful, but I don't see any of that. If the website isn't laid out in a logical way, if things aren't labelled correctly or if links don't really work, then I find it very difficult to move through a process and access the information that I need. Similarly, if an application form isn't correctly labelled or laid out, or if there are sections which involve answering questions, which include things like clicking on parts of the screen or dragging and moving items around into different areas or moving sliders to different positions. These things are really difficult to do when you can't see what's on the screen and can sometimes make it impossible to complete the application. &nbsp;

Some of the barriers that a blind or partially sighted person might face when going for an interview or an in-person chat during an application process are mostly about physical space.

Physical space is very difficult to navigate when you are unfamiliar with an area and you can't see where you are going. So going into an office that I'm unfamiliar with, trying to find a room that I don't know where it is without assistance from people in the space can make it very difficult, if not impossible, to get the best out of the experience for me and impossible to complete the process.

Some other barriers that you could face as someone with a vision impairment during an application process are the formats of any documents that are included. Files sent in PDF formats or anything in a physical format is very difficult to access and can make it impossible to continue the process. So I personally prefer things to be sent in digital formats, in plain text or email, or on a website so that the software that I use is more easily able to read and I'm able to understand the content and make sure that I'm fully prepared and ready to continue to the next part of the process.

### Aschalew: What I need to thrive at work as a visually impaired person

Disability inclusion isn’t just about equipment or policies, it’s about how I’m treated, supported, and valued as an equal member of the team.

Treat me with respectI want the same trust, expectations, and opportunities as any colleague, no pity or exclusion. Value my input, invite me to social events, and remember: disability doesn’t mean inability.

Equal access to opportunitiesPromotions, training, and leadership roles should be based on my skills, not assumptions about my disability.

Support when neededA personal assistant can help with inaccessible documents or navigating unfamiliar spaces, enabling me to work independently.

Accessible tech is essentialScreen readers, braille displays, magnifiers, and OCR scanners are what make work for me possible. Accessibility should be built into my day&nbsp;from day one.

Inclusive communicationI need accessible PDFs, alt text, and alternative formats such as plain text documents&nbsp;so I don’t miss any key information.

Equity over equalitySometimes removing barriers means offering resources to me first.

Ask, don’t assumeI know what works for me as I'm the one experiencing the barriers. Just ask me and be open to adapting.

Accessible spacesClear walkways, consistent layouts, braille signage, and good lighting help me navigate safely and confidently.

Braille For printed agendas or notices, braille ensures I can fully participate.

Final thoughtTrue inclusion is cultural, not just procedural. The adjustments I’ve described are the foundation for a workplace where I can thrive and feel I belong.

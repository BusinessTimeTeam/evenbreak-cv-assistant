# Global language and

Global Language and Legislation 
A best practice guide from Evenbreak 

Introduction 

The words we use and the laws we follow both shape how society views disability. Language can either empower or exclude, and legislation provides the framework that protects rights and sets minimum standards for inclusion. Around the world, disability laws and cultural contexts differ, but one shared principle is that respectful, inclusive language and legal protections are central to creating equity in the workplace. 
 
 
Inclusive global language 

Language is not neutral—terms for disability can reflect stereotypes, stigma, or outdated assumptions. What is considered respectful in one country may feel harmful in another, so organisations working globally need to be attentive to context, culture, and individual preference. Key considerations include: 
Person-first vs. identity-first language – Some countries, (US) prefer "person with a disability" to emphasise that we are people first, and disabling experiences are secondary, whereas others (UK) use social model language (e.g.,"disabled person," "Deaf person") and see disability as an identity and source of pride. Both approaches are valid and individual preference should always be asked and respected. 

Avoid euphemisms – Terms like "differently abled" or "special needs" can feel patronising. Neutral, direct language such as "disabled" or "access requirements" is usually more respectful. 

Living with x – the phrase "living with" or "experiencing" is always more inclusive then dated and negative phrases like "suffers from" or "afflicted by" 

Every day phrases are fine – saying things like "let's go for a walk" to a wheelchair user, or "did you see that program?" to a blind person, won't cause offence - in fact, it's worse to be treading on eggshells for fear of getting it wrong, which often leads to avoiding interactions altogether. 

Recognising that language evolves is important. What was once considered progressive may now be outdated, so staying current and listening to disabled voices is key. 

How legislation shapes perception 

Disability law not only provides rights and protections but also influences how society perceives disability. For example: 

	•	The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA, 1990, USA) framed disability as a civil rights issue, shifting the focus from charity to equity and workplace accommodation. 
	•	The Equality Act (2010, UK) reinforced protections against discrimination and introduced the duty to make "reasonable adjustments" in workplaces and services. 
	•	The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD, 2006) created a global standard, adopted by over 180 countries, promoting disability as a human rights issue and embedding the principles of dignity, participation, and accessibility. 
	•	Regional differences – In some countries, disability laws remain rooted in a medical model, focusing on impairment rather than inclusion. In others, legislation has led to cultural shifts where accessibility is seen as a shared responsibility. 
	•	These legal frameworks shape the expectations workplaces must meet, but they also influence attitudes. Where strong laws exist, there is often greater social awareness and less stigma. 
 
Key considerations for organisations 
	•	Go beyond compliance – Laws establish a baseline, but true inclusion means moving past minimum legal requirements to proactively create accessible, equitable environments. 
	•	Stay globally aware – For international organisations, understand the differences in legislation across regions and aim for consistency by aligning to the highest global standards (such as the UNCRPD). 
	•	Train in respectful language – Provide guidance on inclusive, up-to-date terminology, and encourage staff to listen to how colleagues self-identify. 
	•	Engage with disabled voices – Centre lived experience in policy, communication, and training to ensure both language and legal frameworks are applied with authenticity. 

Top Tips 
For Inclusive Language and Legal Awareness 

	•	Ask, don't assume – Respect individual preferences for how they describe their disability. 
	•	Avoid outdated terms – Words like "handicapped," "crippled," or "invalid" are harmful and should not be used. 
	•	Link language with action – Inclusive wording should be backed up by policies and practices that deliver real accessibility. 
	•	Stay informed – Keep up with evolving legislation and international best practice to ensure compliance and cultural sensitivity. 

 
3 Quick wins for leaders 

	•	Audit your language – Review HR documents, job ads, and policies for outdated or exclusionary terms, and replace them with inclusive alternatives. 
	•	Align to global best practice – Even if local law is limited, set company-wide standards that reflect international frameworks like the UNCRPD. 
	•	Lead by example – Use inclusive language in all communications, correct harmful terms when they arise, and model respect in every interaction.

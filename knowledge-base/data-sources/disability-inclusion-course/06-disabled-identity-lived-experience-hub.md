### Testimonial: Ruby's experience

We asked Ruby to share her honest experience of navigating work and recruitment as a disabled person.

From job applications to onboarding, and everyday team dynamics, Ruby offered reflections on what works, what doesn’t, and what inclusion really looks like in practice.

### "I'm Not Fragile": A Q&A on Disability and Inclusion

Navigate through the carousel to see what Ruby has to say!

### Summary

Inclusion means listening, learning, and acting.Ruby’s experiences show how small adjustments and genuine understanding can transform the workplace.

### Question: What are some of the biggest worries or barriers you face when applying for jobs?

Answer:

- People assuming I'm not worth hiring because of the "problems" they think come with hiring disabled people.

- Ruling out lots of jobs because I just don’t think they’ll be practical for me due to my disability.

- Being treated like I’m not intelligent or can’t understand things - just because I’m disabled.

- The assumption that I shouldn’t work or that my disability automatically makes me less capable.

### Question: Are there parts of the recruitment process that feel especially challenging?

Answer:Yes - so many! Things like:

- Application forms asking for way too much unnecessary info at the application stage&nbsp;(e.g. full addresses and phone numbers for old jobs, exact leaving dates).

- Having to create multiple accounts - it’s hard to keep track of what I’ve applied for.

- Job descriptions not saying if they’re Disability Confident or if adjustments are even an option.

- Worrying about whether the process will be accessible or if asking for support will harm my chances. I sometimes feel like I'm being a burden just for asking.

### Question: What helps you feel confident and comfortable when starting a new role?

Answer:

- Having a single point of contact for the first couple of weeks really helps.

- Getting a tour before starting - especially of things like accessible toilets.

- Knowing that adaptations are already in place and that Access to Work is sorted.

- A friendly team, where I don’t feel singled out or treated like the “different” one.

### Question: What adjustments or ways of working have helped you thrive?

Answer:

- Being able to take toilet breaks when I need them.

- An extra 5 minutes here and there just to reset - disability takes extra energy and time, and sometimes I need that buffer.

### Question: What do you wish managers or colleagues better understood about your experience?

Answer:

- I’m not fragile. If I’m doing something wrong, I can take honest feedback. Just acknowledge that my disability might make some things harder, and that I’m working through that.

- Don’t be afraid to ask what could help me. If something’s not working, I'd rather&nbsp;talk about it.

### Question: Are there any small changes that could make a big difference in helping you feel included and supported?

Answer:

- Having things like accessible toilets and ramps already in place.

- A quick check-in or chat - just someone asking how things are going makes a huge difference.

### Video transcript: Adam's lived experience

Adam:

I want to talk to you about the importance of not making assumptions when it comes to understanding someone's access needs. So for me and my lived experience, I am impacted by cerebral palsy, I am a wheelchair user, and my voice is different.

So I think it's fair to say that when people meet me they will often notice this difference and therefore because my differences are quite visible, I think with the best intentions will presume that my physical access is the biggest barrier that I face, whereas actually, I also have hidden differences. I'm impacted by dyslexia and anxiety.

I think what's really interesting is because these differences and because these barriers are hidden, people will make an assumption. Again, with often the best intention about what my access needs are and quite often they'll ask me if there's anything else I need because there's assumptions that they can see what my need are.

So I guess the important thing here is to remember not to to make assumptions about individual access needs, and make sure that we have open conversations so we make sure we are supporting the whole individual.

### Video transcript: Rox's lived experience

Rox:

Hi, I'm Rox, and in this video I want to share with you a little bit about my lived experience. So I was born with cerebral palsy, spastic dysplasia of the upper and the limbs. I also manage multiple mental health conditions, including depression, anxiety, OCD, and sometimes panic attacks. I also identify with the fact that I face barriers due to a level of neurodiversity, but I'm yet to get a diagnosis.

You may hear me call myself neurodiverse pattern pending instead. So what does this mean on really high level terms? In practical terms, it means I utilise a manual wheelchair. I benefit from the support of a PA to remain independent. And I love a bit of assistive technology. How has this created barriers for me in the recruitment process or indeed in the workspace?

Well, I faced a number of barriers in different spaces, but here are just some examples. Standard, as you'd expect, the physical access barriers, getting to a job site and realising actually, there's no step free access. There's no lift, there's only stairs. Having no access to an accessible toilet, only a ground floor toilet, potentially, and no information on accessible parking.

But there's also things that other people may seem as small but actually make a big difference. For example, how furniture can create physical access barriers. I can tell you it is really hard to feel like an equal in a job interview or a team meeting when everyone sat at the table is sat at a table big enough for me to practically roll under and create a fort.

So [00:02:00] yeah, you don't feel like an equal in that kind of space. Then there's things such as information barriers. I might see a job advert that I'm really, really interested in, but I also know that I have to go there three or four times a week, so I need to know if it's accessible. There's no information on your website.

There's no one to contact or contact information, so I just don't know. Then there's other kind of barriers, which include communication barriers. And that might be the way that we have to convey experience in an interview, for example, when there's a written exam and you've even contacted in advance about utilising a laptop, but the interviewers haven't got that information, so it hasn't been passed on. So I've had to literally try and write with a pen and paper to do a written assessment. And unfortunately, that has created some really bad, not only communication barriers for me, but actual anxiety, which I think takes us nicely on to one of the biggest barriers I think most people face, but also not one of the easiest to unpick, absolutely not, but one that we absolutely all can, and that's attitudinal barriers.

Once I was very successful, or seemingly so, at an interview and I got brought back in, told how great I was, and then came the but. They said, we work with aging clientele, I don't think they'd understand or feel comfortable with you in a face-to-face role. And what happens is it then created additional attitudinal barriers for me, internalising whether I should apply for future jobs, whether I'd be wanted there, whether I'm needed there. So actually other people's attitudes can internalise and create future attitudinal barriers for me, and it absolutely has done so. So honestly, one of the biggest things that I found helpful though, is attitude, is the support of managers and colleagues. Having those open conversations, a workplace culture that doesn't hone in on me because I'm disabled, but actually acknowledges I have a great skill set and what does everyone need to work at their best? And opening up those conversations so I can explore what adjustments I need.

And through those kind of cultures, I found things like flexible working's been really helpful so that I can manage my workload based on my pain levels. Having hybrid working, so there may be some days I don't have to come into the office, use the energy for travel, or I don't have to worry if a PA's called in sick because I can still access work. In regards to things like meetings, being able to join meetings online, which means I can use captions to process information. And I can just ask, okay, you've put me on a project, can I have a meeting with you rather than trying to process and decipher this very large email and having to use that energy to email back when I could have a quick five minute catch up and ask those clarifying conversations. It not only makes it more accessible for me, but actually, surely it makes me a more productive and effective employee.

So I really hope me sharing a bit about my lived experience has helped you consider what might help you, how you can help other people, and what workplace culture really looks like or really should look like. Thank you for listening.

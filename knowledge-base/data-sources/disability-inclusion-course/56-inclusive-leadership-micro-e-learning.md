Let's be honest, some conversations are hard. Talking about disability, mental health, neurodivergence or adjustments can feel sensitive and awkward if you're not used to it. You might worry about saying the wrong thing or not having the answers or even making things worse. But in reality, you don't need to be perfect.

You just need to be present. Inclusive leadership starts with being human, not with having or following a script. You are often the first port of call, the first person someone comes to when they need support, not HR, not a policy, you, a manager. Whether someone wants to talk about adjustments, disability, or something entirely different that's impacting their work, how you respond will shape how safe and comfortable they feel moving forward. ### What kind of leader do you want to be?

Scroll through the following slides for our ultimate tips on considerations, reflections, and practices that you can use in your leadership style, that will set an inclusive tone for your team.

### Summary

Being a good leader isn't about knowing the answers, getting it right, or being a perfect representation of your company. Being a leader is about being open and honest about your experiences, acting graciously when mistakes are made, and ultimately about valuing your team members&nbsp;as people before employees.

### Clearly express your intent

Whether you’re offering support, addressing a concern, or seeking clarity, clearly stating your intent at the start of a courageous conversation helps build trust and set a respectful tone. &nbsp;

It lets the other person know why you're initiating the conversation, which can ease anxiety and reduce the risk of misunderstanding. &nbsp;

It doesn’t need to be complicated - a simple “I want to check in because I value working with you” or “I’d like us to talk about something that matters so we can work well together” can go a long way in creating the right environment for honest dialogue.&nbsp;

### Listen actively

Practicing listening - really listening.&nbsp;That means putting down any assumptions and giving someone your full attention. No multitasking here.&nbsp;

Sometimes people just need to share their thoughts – not find solutions or receive advice. We often forget about or dismiss the power of listening and how difficult yet transformational it can be. &nbsp;

### Ask open-ended questions

When having conversations about inclusion and access needs, let your curiosity guide you, not your fear of getting it wrong. This means asking thoughtful, open-ended questions such as:&nbsp;

- What would you like to talk about today?&nbsp;

- What else would be helpful for me to know?&nbsp;

- What would help you right now?&nbsp;

- How can I support you best?&nbsp;

- What do you need or not need from me today?&nbsp;

### The individual is the expert

The individual is the expert in their lived experience. They know what works for them and what doesn’t.&nbsp;

Your job isn’t to take over or offer unsolicited advice. It’s to listen, ask respectful questions, offer support, and most importantly follow through.&nbsp;

If you say you’ll check something, check it. If you say you’ll implement something, do it. That will have a positive impact for them and for the whole team too.&nbsp;

### Role modelling

Disability inclusion, and inclusion in general, isn’t just what you say, it’s what you do.&nbsp;

Role modelling means showing that it’s okay to be human. That it's okay to need help, take breaks, or not be okay every single day.&nbsp;

When you talk openly, appropriately, about your own challenges or boundaries, you’re showing your team that vulnerability is welcome here.&nbsp;

So, whether it's setting boundaries on work time, talking about wellbeing, or simply admitting “I don’t know but I’ll find out,” you’re shaping a culture of inclusion by example.&nbsp;

### Know yourself, create your style

There’s no one-size-fits-all approach to inclusive leadership. But knowing yourself is a great place to start.&nbsp;

Reflect on your own values, communication style, and biases. Think about how you respond under pressure, how you give feedback, and how you receive it.&nbsp;

Then, build a leadership style that’s authentic to you and inclusive of others.&nbsp;

And when you inevitably get it wrong? That’s okay. Own it, apologize, and learn from it. Then, move forward by applying what you’ve learned along the way. And do your best not to repeat the same mistakes.&nbsp;

## Text: heading paragraph

### Top Tips

There is an optional voice over below.Here are our five top tips for best practice that you can start implementing today in order to role model inclusive behaviour and accessible practices for your team:

## List: numbered

Ask, don't assume - check in on a one to one basis with each of your employees regularly, and ask what they need to thrive in their roles, rather than assuming what support they need.

Normalise flexibility - whether this is about flexible working hours, tailoring people's roles to suit their strengths and interests, or finding new ways of working with your team, adaptability is key.

Hold yourself accountable - say what you mean, and mean what you say. If you have said you will do something, follow through with it, to ensure that your team sees you as a reliable and intentional leader.

Model vulnerability - share your own experiences and your journey to leadership, especially when you got things wrong. This teaches your team that it's safe to make mistakes, and builds a culture of openness.

Share information in a variety of ways - including alternative formats for documents, written or verbal options depending on individual preference, and clear, structured instructions and goals. When we share information openly, everyone has informed choices, and can be part of shaping decisions and cultures.

Create a live written resource for all employees that documents conversations about access needs, adjustments made, and things that have worked/not worked. This should be confidential and collaborative, so that both you and&nbsp;individual can refer back to it and update it when needed.

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### What is psychological safety in the workplace?

Correct answer! Psychological safety means people feel safe to express themselves and contribute ideas openly.

- **Correct answer:** A. When employees feel free to speak up, share ideas, and ask for help without fear of judgement or repercussion.

- Answer option: B. When employees work independently without needing feedback.

- Answer option: C. When leaders avoid discussing mistakes or challenges.

- Answer option: D. When only senior staff are encouraged to share opinions

## Knowledgecheck: multiple choice

### Which of the following leadership practices best promotes inclusivity and trust?

Correct answer! Inclusive leaders “ask, don’t assume” — open one-to-one conversations help identify individual needs and build mutual trust.

- Answer option: A. Assuming what team members need based on past experience.

- Answer option: B. Keeping decisions private to maintain authority.

- Answer option: C. Correcting mistakes publicly to encourage accountability.

- **Correct answer:** D. Asking team members individually what support they need to thrive.
